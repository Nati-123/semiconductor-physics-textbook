Connects to Chapter 7: An intrinsic semiconductor's only carriers come from rare thermal bond-breaking, giving silicon a room-temperature intrinsic carrier concentration of roughly \(n_i\approx10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) — tiny compared to the roughly \(5\times10^{22}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) silicon atoms themselves. An extrinsic semiconductor adds donor or acceptor atoms that contribute carriers without needing thermal excitation at all, routinely reaching \(10^{15}\)–\(10^{18}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) — a difference of many orders of magnitude that Chapter 9 makes precise.

Challenge Questions

1. Why does the extrinsic lattice have a free electron immediately, without needing to break a bond?

A donor atom (Group V) has 5 valence electrons — 4 are used in covalent bonds like silicon, and the 5th is only loosely bound, becoming a free carrier at ordinary temperatures without needing to break any covalent bond.

Count the valence electrons on the donor atom compared to silicon's 4.

2. Roughly how many orders of magnitude larger is a typical extrinsic (doped) carrier concentration compared to silicon's intrinsic carrier concentration at room temperature?

Silicon's intrinsic carrier concentration (~10^10 cm^-3) is dwarfed by typical doping levels (~10^15-10^18 cm^-3) — a difference of 5 to 8 orders of magnitude, which is why doping so dramatically controls conductivity.
