Connects to Chapter 1: This diagram illustrates the boxed Coulomb's Law equation, \(F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}\), and the sign rule described just above it — repulsive when \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) share a sign, attractive when they differ.

What Each Control Does

Charge combination
Switches between like-positive, like-negative, and unlike charge pairs — this changes only the sign in Coulomb's Law, which flips the force from repulsive to attractive.
Separation r
Changes the distance between the two charges — the force arrows shrink following the \(1/r^2\) inverse-square dependence, not a straight-line decrease.

Challenge Questions

1. If you select "Unlike Charges (+, −)", which direction do the force arrows point?

Unlike charges attract, so both arrows point toward the opposite charge.

Try selecting "Unlike Charges" in the simulation above and look at the arrow directions.

2. Coulomb's Law says force is proportional to \(1/r^2\). If you double the separation \(r\), what happens to the force magnitude?

Since \(F \propto 1/r^2\), doubling \(r\) means dividing \(F\) by \(2^2 = 4\).

Remember: the exponent on \(r\) is 2, so distance changes are squared in their effect on force.

3. Which charge combination(s) in this simulation are repulsive?

Any pair of charges sharing the same sign repels — it doesn't matter whether both are positive or both are negative.

Check both like-charge options in the simulation — do they both push apart?

4. For the same pair of charges, which separation produces the larger force arrow?

Smaller separation means a larger force, since force falls off as \(1/r^2\) — closer charges exert (and feel) a stronger force.

Slide r to 1 and then to 5 in the simulation and compare the arrow lengths directly.
