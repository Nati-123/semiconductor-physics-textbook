What to Observe

  • Three curves span many orders of magnitude, so both axes are log-scaled — a straight line on this chart means a power-law relationship, not a linear one.
  • The SRH curve (purple) has a constant slope of 1 (linear in Δn); the Auger curve (red) has a slope of 3 (cubic in Δn) and eventually overtakes every other mechanism if Δn goes high enough.
  • The dashed marker line shows exactly where each curve sits at your chosen Δn, and the readout below reports which mechanism dominates there, and by how many times.

Guided Exploration

  • Predict: Before switching from silicon to GaAs, predict whether the direct-recombination curve will shift up or down relative to SRH.
  • Try this: With silicon selected, drag the Δn marker from low to high and watch which curve is highest — and how the dominance ratio in the readout changes — at each injection level.
  • Observe: Switching from silicon to GaAs shifts the direct-recombination curve up dramatically, since GaAs is a direct-gap material where band-to-band recombination is efficient.
  • Explore: Find the Δn value where the Auger curve crosses above SRH — that crossover marks where the high-injection Δn³ term starts to matter.

Connects to Chapter 13: Several distinct mechanisms return excess carriers to equilibrium. Direct recombination (efficient in direct-gap materials like GaAs) involves an electron and hole recombining straight across the gap; indirect recombination in materials like silicon requires a momentum-conserving assist, making it inherently slower. Trap-assisted recombination, formalized by Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) theory, dominates in most indirect-gap devices via defect levels in the gap. Auger recombination, growing as \(\Delta n^3\), only becomes significant at very high injection — which is exactly why the low-level-injection assumption of a single, Δn-independent lifetime \(\tau\) (used elsewhere in this chapter) breaks down at high injection.

What Each Control Does

Material
Switches between silicon (indirect gap, weak direct recombination) and GaAs (direct gap, strong direct recombination) — shifts all three curves.
Δn marker
Moves the vertical marker line across the fixed Δn sweep range; the dominant-mechanism readout always describes the marker's position, not the whole curve.
Doping exponent
Sets the equilibrium majority carrier concentration \(N\), which shifts the direct-recombination curve (it depends on \(n_0+p_0+\Delta n\)).

Challenge Questions

1. Which recombination mechanism's rate grows fastest as excess carrier concentration Δn increases?

Auger recombination scales as Δn³, so it grows fastest and only dominates at very high injection levels.

Slide the Δn marker to its maximum value and see which curve is highest there.

2. On this log-log chart, why does the SRH curve look like a straight line with a shallower slope than the Auger curve?

Taking log₁₀ of a power law Δn^k turns it into a straight line of slope k on a log-log chart — that's exactly why SRH (k=1) and Auger (k=3) have visibly different slopes here.

Take log₁₀ of both sides of R_SRH=Δn/τ_SRH and R_Auger=C·Δn³ and compare the resulting slopes in log(Δn).

Key Takeaway: No single recombination mechanism dominates everywhere — which one controls the carrier lifetime depends on both the material (direct- vs. indirect-gap) and the injection level, because each mechanism has a different power-law dependence on Δn (SRH linear, direct roughly linear-to-quadratic, Auger cubic). At the low-level injection typically assumed elsewhere in this chapter, SRH (or direct recombination in direct-gap materials) usually dominates; Auger only takes over at very high injection, which is precisely where the constant-τ approximation used in the rest of this chapter stops being accurate.
