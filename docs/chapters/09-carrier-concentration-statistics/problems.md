### Problem 11

### Problem 14

### Problem 16

Why does the Boltzmann approximation fail for a degenerate semiconductor (Chapter 8)?

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">In a degenerate semiconductor, the Fermi level sits inside or above the conduction band, so many conduction-band states have E-E_F comparable to or less than k_BT — the condition E-E_F ≫ k_BT required for the Boltzmann approximation is violated, and the full Fermi-Dirac integral must be used instead.</p>
</div>
</details>

## Difficult

### Problem 17

Starting from \(n_0=N_Ce^{-(E_C-E_F)/k_BT}\) and \(p_0=N_Ve^{-(E_F-E_V)/k_BT}\), derive the mass action law algebraically, showing each step.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Multiplying: <span class="arithmatex">\(n_0p_0=N_CN_V\,e^{-(E_C-E_F)/k_BT}\,e^{-(E_F-E_V)/k_BT}=N_CN_V\,e^{-[(E_C-E_F)+(E_F-E_V)]/k_BT}\)</span>. The exponent simplifies to <span class="arithmatex">\(-(E_C-E_V)/k_BT=-E_g/k_BT\)</span>, with E_F cancelling exactly, giving <span class="arithmatex">\(n_0p_0=N_CN_V\,e^{-E_g/k_BT}\)</span>. Since this equals <span class="arithmatex">\(n_i^2\)</span> by definition (setting n0=p0=ni in the intrinsic case gives the same expression), the mass action law <span class="arithmatex">\(n_0p_0=n_i^2\)</span> follows.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 18

A hypothetical semiconductor has \(N_C=1\times10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), \(N_V=1\times10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), and is doped so heavily n-type that \(N_D=8\times10^{18}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) (fully ionized, no acceptors). Using the degenerate-regime preview formula from Chapter 8, \(E_C-E_F=k_BT\ln(N_C/N_D)\), estimate \(E_C-E_F\) at 300 K, and comment on whether the non-degenerate approximation used throughout this chapter is still reasonably valid.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;"><span class="arithmatex">\(E_C-E_F=(0.0259)\ln(1\times10^{19}/8\times10^{18})=(0.0259)\ln(1.25)\approx(0.0259)(0.223)\approx0.0058\ \text{eV}\)</span>. Since this is much smaller than a few times k_BT (0.0259 eV), the Fermi level is essentially at the edge of "a few k_BT" from E_C — right at the boundary where the non-degenerate/Boltzmann approximation used throughout this chapter begins to break down, consistent with this doping level being close to N_C itself.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 19

Two non-degenerate silicon samples, A and B, are both at 300 K. Sample A has \(n_0=10^{15}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\); Sample B has \(n_0=10^{17}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). Compare their \(n_0p_0\) products, and explain what stays the same and what differs between the two samples.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Both samples are silicon at the same temperature, so both have the identical n_i and therefore the identical n0p0 = ni^2 product (≈9.3×10^19 cm^-6), despite their very different individual n0 values. What differs is p0 individually: Sample A has p0 = ni^2/10^15 ≈ 9.3×10^4 cm^-3, while Sample B has a smaller p0 = ni^2/10^17 ≈ 930 cm^-3 — the heavier-doped sample (B) has a correspondingly more strongly suppressed minority carrier concentration, even though the product itself is unchanged.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 20

Explain, in your own words, why the charge neutrality condition alone is not enough to solve for n0 and p0, and why the mass action law is needed as a second equation. What physical assumption does the mass action law itself depend on that would make this two-equation approach invalid?

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Charge neutrality is a single equation relating n0 and p0 (given known, fixed dopant concentrations), but it has two unknowns — infinitely many (n0, p0) pairs could satisfy it alone. The mass action law supplies the needed second, independent relationship between the same two unknowns, so the two equations together can be solved uniquely. The mass action law itself depends on the non-degenerate (Boltzmann) approximation used to derive n0 and p0 in the first place — in a degenerate semiconductor (Chapter 8), where this approximation fails, n0p0 = ni^2 is no longer exactly true, and this two-equation approach would need to be replaced with the full Fermi-Dirac integrals.</p>
</div>
</details>
