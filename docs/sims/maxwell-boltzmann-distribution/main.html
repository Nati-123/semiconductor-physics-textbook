What Each Control Does

Temperature slider
Sets the gas temperature from 100 K to 1000 K — this single number controls both the width of the curve and the average kinetic energy, via \(\langle KE\rangle = \tfrac{3}{2}k_BT\).
Room Temperature (300 K) button
Jumps straight to \(T=300\) K, the reference temperature used throughout this course for \(k_BT\approx 0.0259\) eV.
Liquid Nitrogen (77 K) button
Jumps to a common cryogenic reference temperature, so you can directly contrast it against room temperature.

Challenge Questions

1. As temperature increases from 100 K to 1000 K, what happens to the most probable speed \(v_p\)?

\(v_p=\sqrt{2k_BT/m}\) grows with temperature, so the curve's peak shifts right as T rises.

Drag the slider from low to high and watch the dashed \(v_p\) line.

2. At any given temperature, which speed marker sits farther to the right?

\(v_{rms}=\sqrt{3k_BT/m}\) is always larger than \(v_p=\sqrt{2k_BT/m}\), since 3 > 2 inside the square root, regardless of T.

Compare the two dashed lines at any single temperature setting.

3. If temperature doubles, does the average kinetic energy \(\langle KE\rangle = \tfrac{3}{2}k_BT\) also exactly double?

\(\langle KE\rangle\) is directly proportional to \(T\) (no exponent), so doubling T exactly doubles the average kinetic energy.

Set T=300, note the KE readout, then set T=600 and compare directly.

4. Comparing the two presets, which has the wider (more spread out) distribution curve?

Higher temperature means particles have a wider range of likely speeds, so the 300 K curve is broader than the 77 K curve.

Click both presets back to back and compare the curve widths directly.

5. Why does this classical gas result matter for a semiconductor, which is a solid, not a gas?

As the chapter text notes, the statistical framework developed for gases transfers directly to electrons in a crystal, which is why temperature appears throughout later carrier-statistics chapters.

Re-read the paragraph just above this MicroSim in the chapter.
