Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

Try This

  • Try this: With EF at its default 1.00 eV and T = 300 K, find where the purple curve crosses the dashed f = 0.5 line. Confirm it happens exactly at E = EF, marked by the red dot.
  • Predict: Before dragging the EF slider to the left, predict which way the whole S-curve will shift.
  • Observe: Drag EF left and right and confirm the curve translates with it — the crossing point f(EF) = 0.5 always follows EF, but the curve's shape never changes.
  • Switch Temperature presets from 0 K to 77 K to 300 K to 600 K. At 0 K the purple curve should hug the faint gray step-function reference almost exactly; at 600 K it should be visibly more gradual, spread over a much wider energy range.
  • Read the numbers: Compare kBT in the readout at 77 K versus 600 K, and connect the size of kBT to how "smeared" the curve looks around EF.

Connects to Chapter 6: This MicroSim plots the Fermi-Dirac distribution \(f(E) = 1/(1+\exp((E-E_F)/k_BT))\) directly. By construction, \(f(E_F)=1/(1+\exp(0))=1/2\) at every temperature — the defining property of the Fermi level. As \(T\to0\), the exponential becomes infinitely steep and \(f(E)\) collapses into the sharp step function shown as a faint gray reference, recovering the \(T=0\) **Fermi energy** picture: every state below \(E_F\) full, every state above empty. This is the same distribution the Density of States and Fermi Level Explorer uses to shade occupied vs. unoccupied states on top of a real band diagram.

What Each Control Does

Fermi level EF slider
Sets \(E_F\) in the Fermi-Dirac distribution; the entire S-curve translates so it always crosses \(f=0.5\) at \(E=E_F\).
Temperature preset dropdown
Jumps the temperature slider directly to 0 K, 77 K (liquid nitrogen), 300 K (room temperature), or 600 K.
Temperature T (K) slider
Sets the absolute temperature, converted internally to thermal energy \(k_BT\); higher T makes the curve's transition region wider and more gradual.

Challenge Questions

1. At any temperature above absolute zero, what is f(E) exactly at E = EF?

Substituting E = EF gives f(EF) = 1/(1+exp(0)) = 1/(1+1) = 0.5, exactly, at any temperature and for any material — this is the defining property of the Fermi level.

Look at the red dot on the curve — it always sits on the dashed f = 0.5 line, directly above the EF label.

2. As T → 0 K, what does the Fermi-Dirac curve approach?

As T → 0, kBT → 0 and the exponential becomes infinitely steep, collapsing f(E) into a sharp step: every state below EF is fully occupied, every state above is fully empty — the T=0 Fermi energy picture.

Select the "0 K (approx.)" temperature preset and compare the purple curve to the faint gray step-function reference.

3. If you increase EF while keeping T fixed, what happens to f(E) at a fixed energy E just below the old EF?

Raising EF shifts the whole S-curve to the right (toward higher E). A fixed energy E that used to sit close to the transition now sits further into the "below EF" region, where occupation is higher, so f(E) increases.

Pick a fixed x-position on the plot and watch the curve's height there as you drag the EF slider up.

4. Comparing T = 77 K to T = 600 K at the same EF, how does the width of the transition region (where f(E) changes from near 1 to near 0) compare?

The transition region has a width of order a few kBT. Since kBT is proportional to T, the 600 K curve smears its transition over a much wider energy range than the 77 K curve.

Switch between the 77 K and 600 K presets and compare how "spread out" the S-curve looks around EF in each case.
