### Problem 18

Compare the fraction of light absorbed within \(1.5\ \mu\text{m}\) for \(\alpha=2\times10^{3}\ \text{cm}^{-1}\) and \(\alpha=8\times10^{3}\ \text{cm}^{-1}\).

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Fraction absorbed \(=1-e^{-\alpha x}\). At \(\alpha=2\times10^3\ \text{cm}^{-1}\): \(1-e^{-(2\times10^3)(1.5\times10^{-4})}=1-e^{-0.3}\approx25.9\%\). At \(\alpha=8\times10^3\ \text{cm}^{-1}\): \(1-e^{-1.2}\approx69.9\%\) — quadrupling \(\alpha\) absorbs nearly three times as much light in the same thickness.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 19

Compare generation current \(I_{gen}\) for a silicon diode with \(W=1\ \mu\text{m}\), \(A=1\times10^{-2}\ \text{cm}^2\), at \(\tau_0=0.5\ \mu\text{s}\) versus \(\tau_0=5\ \mu\text{s}\).

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">At \(\tau_0=0.5\ \mu\text{s}\): \(G_{th}=(1.5\times10^{10})/(5\times10^{-7})=3\times10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\text{s}^{-1}\), \(I_{gen}\approx4.81\times10^{-9}\ \text{A}\). At \(\tau_0=5\ \mu\text{s}\): \(I_{gen}\approx4.81\times10^{-10}\ \text{A}\), exactly ten times smaller — since \(I_{gen}\propto1/\tau_0\), a ten-fold longer generation lifetime gives a ten-fold smaller leakage current, which is why controlling defect density (and hence \(\tau_0\)) matters directly for diode leakage specifications.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 20

Silicon photodiodes are widely used and highly effective light sensors despite silicon being an indirect-gap material, yet silicon LEDs are impractical. Explain why absorption (detection) and emission are affected so differently by the direct/indirect distinction.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Absorption in an indirect-gap material is less efficient than in a direct-gap material at any single wavelength, but a photodiode can simply be made thick enough (or operated well above the band gap, where phonon-assisted absorption becomes much stronger) to absorb nearly all incident light regardless — thickness compensates for a smaller \(\alpha\), with no fundamental efficiency ceiling. Radiative recombination for emission has no equivalent compensation: every recombination event competes directly against much faster non-radiative pathways (trap-assisted/SRH, Chapter 13), so an indirect-gap material's radiative efficiency stays low no matter how the device is designed, since there is no "more device volume" fix for a process that is inherently outcompeted electron-by-electron.</p>
</div>
</details>
