### Problem 15

A silicon sample at 300 K has \(n_0=3\times10^{17}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) and \(p_0\) negligible, with \(\mu_L=1350\ \text{cm}^2/\text{V·s}\) and \(\mu_I=650\ \text{cm}^2/\text{V·s}\) at this doping. Find the conductivity and resistivity.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">

### Problem 18

Using the Matthiessen's rule model \(\mu_L\propto T^{-3/2}\) and \(\mu_I\propto T^{3/2}\) (fixed doping), explain what happens to the *ratio* \(\mu_L/\mu_I\) as temperature increases, and what this implies about which mechanism dominates at high vs. low temperature.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Since <span class="arithmatex">\(\mu_L\propto T^{-3/2}\)</span> and <span class="arithmatex">\(\mu_I\propto T^{3/2}\)</span>, the ratio <span class="arithmatex">\(\mu_L/\mu_I\propto T^{-3}\)</span>, which shrinks rapidly as T increases. A shrinking ratio means μ_L becomes small relative to μ_I at high T — so lattice scattering (the smaller mobility) dominates at high temperature. Conversely, at low T, the ratio grows large, meaning μ_I becomes the smaller mobility, so impurity scattering dominates at low temperature — consistent with the physical picture of each mechanism.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 19

A resistor must have exactly \(R=2500\ \Omega\) using a film with \(R_s=500\ \Omega/\square\). If the width is fixed at \(W=15\ \mu\text{m}\), find the required length L.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Number of squares needed: <span class="arithmatex">\(L/W = R/R_s = 2500/500 = 5\)</span>. So <span class="arithmatex">\(L = 5\times W = 5\times15 = 75\ \mu\text{m}\)</span>.</p>
</div>

### Problem 20

Two silicon samples at 300 K have \(N_{D,1}=2\times10^{14}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) (very lightly doped, \(\mu_{n,1}\approx1400\ \text{cm}^2/\text{V·s}\)) and \(N_{D,2}=5\times10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) (very heavily doped, \(\mu_{n,2}\approx80\ \text{cm}^2/\text{V·s}\)). Compute the ratio of their conductivities, and explain whether heavier doping "always" increases conductivity based on this comparison.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;"><span class="arithmatex">\(\sigma_1\propto N_{D,1}\mu_{n,1}=(2\times10^{14})(1400)=2.8\times10^{17}\)</span>. <span class="arithmatex">\(\sigma_2\propto N_{D,2}\mu_{n,2}=(5\times10^{19})(80)=4.0\times10^{21}\)</span>. Ratio <span class="arithmatex">\(\sigma_2/\sigma_1\approx14300\)</span> — conductivity is still much higher for the heavier-doped sample, despite its mobility being over 17× smaller. In this comparison heavier doping does still increase conductivity overall, but the huge mobility reduction (from 1400 down to 80, nearly a 95% drop) shows that at extremely heavy doping the conductivity gain per additional dopant atom becomes much smaller than it would be from the concentration increase alone — a diminishing-returns effect worth noting even though conductivity does not actually reverse direction in this example.</p>
</div>
</details>
