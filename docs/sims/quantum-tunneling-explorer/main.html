Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

Try This

  • Try this: Set the barrier width to its minimum and read the transmission probability \(T\); then set it to its maximum and compare.
  • Predict: Before raising \(V_0/E\) from 1.1 to 5, predict whether \(T\) will go up or down.
  • Observe: Watch how the decaying curve inside the barrier gets steeper as \(V_0/E\) increases.
  • Explain: Explain why a nonzero transmitted wave exists on the right side even though classically the particle should never cross a barrier taller than its energy.

Connects to Chapter 2: This MicroSim visualizes the boxed quantum-tunneling approximation \(T \approx e^{-2\kappa L}\), \(\kappa=\sqrt{2m_e(V_0-E)}/\hbar\) from the chapter, holding the particle energy fixed at \(E=1\) eV and using \(\hbar=1.0546\times10^{-34}\) J·s and \(m_e=9.109\times10^{-31}\) kg. This approximation is only accurate when \(\kappa L \gg 1\) (a "thick" or "tall" barrier); for very thin or short barriers, treat the numeric \(T\) as illustrative rather than exact.

What Each Control Does

V₀/E ratio slider
Sets the barrier height as a multiple of the fixed particle energy (1 eV) — must exceed 1 for classically forbidden tunneling to apply.
Barrier width L slider
Sets the barrier thickness from 0.1 nm to 2 nm — wider barriers make the exponential decay inside the barrier much more severe.

Challenge Questions

1. If you double the barrier width \(L\) (keeping \(V_0/E\) fixed), what happens to \(T\)?

Since \(T\approx e^{-2\kappa L}\), doubling \(L\) squares the exponential decay factor, causing a dramatic drop in transmission probability.

Look at where \(L\) sits in the exponent of \(T \approx e^{-2\kappa L}\).

2. As \(V_0/E\) increases (barrier gets taller relative to particle energy), what happens to \(\kappa\) and \(T\)?

\(\kappa=\sqrt{2m_e(V_0-E)}/\hbar\) grows as \(V_0-E\) grows, and a larger \(\kappa\) makes the exponential decay in \(T\approx e^{-2\kappa L}\) steeper, shrinking T.

Drag the V₀/E slider from 1.1 toward 5 and watch both the decay curve steepness and the T readout.

3. Inside the barrier region, why does the wavefunction amplitude decay exponentially instead of oscillating?

Classically forbidden regions (V0 > E) turn the Schrödinger equation's oscillatory solution into a decaying exponential, since the wavenumber becomes imaginary.

Compare the sign of (V0 - E) inside the barrier to outside it.

4. What does a smaller-amplitude oscillating wave to the right of the barrier represent?

The reduced-amplitude wave on the far side represents the transmitted wave; its amplitude relative to the incident wave sets the transmission probability T.

Recall which side of the barrier the incident wave starts on, and which region represents "after" the barrier.

5. This simulation's \(T\approx e^{-2\kappa L}\) formula is described in the panel as an approximation. Under what condition is it most accurate?

The simplified exponential form drops correction terms that only become negligible when kappa*L is large; for very thin or short barriers the full transmission formula differs noticeably.
