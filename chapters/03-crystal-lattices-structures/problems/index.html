    End-of-Chapter Problems: Crystal Lattices and Structures

    Work through these problems to reinforce your understanding of the crystal lattices and structures covered in Chapter 3.

    Easy

    Problem 1

    Define, in one sentence each, the difference between a crystal lattice and a crystal structure.

    Show Solution

    A crystal lattice is an infinite, purely geometric array of points with no atoms attached. A crystal structure is what results when a basis (one or more atoms) is attached to every point of that lattice.

    Problem 2

    What is the defining property of a primitive cell, and how many lattice points does it contain?

    Show Solution

    A primitive cell is the smallest possible repeating volume that tiles all of space to reproduce the full lattice. It contains exactly one lattice point (counting shared points by the fraction actually inside the cell).

    Problem 3

    State the lattice constant of silicon, with units.

    Show Solution

    \(a = 0.543\ \text{nm}\).

    Problem 4

    How many atoms per conventional unit cell does the simple cubic structure contain? Show the shared-atom counting explicitly.

    Show Solution

    8 corner atoms, each shared among 8 cells: \(8\times\tfrac18 = 1\) atom per cell.

    Problem 5

    State the coordination numbers of the simple cubic, body-centered cubic, and face-centered cubic structures.

    Show Solution

    Simple cubic: 6. Body-centered cubic: 8. Face-centered cubic: 12.

    Problem 6

    How many atoms per conventional unit cell does the diamond lattice structure contain?

    Show Solution

    8 atoms per conventional cell, from two interpenetrating FCC sublattices each contributing 4 atoms.

    Problem 7

    In one sentence, what is the key structural difference between the diamond and zincblende structures?

    Show Solution

    Diamond has the same atomic species on both interpenetrating FCC sublattices, while zincblende has two different atomic species, one on each sublattice.

    Problem 8

    A crystal plane never intercepts the \(y\) or \(z\) axes and intercepts the \(x\) axis at \(1a\). What are its Miller indices?

    Show Solution

    Reciprocals: \(1/1=1\), \(1/\infty=0\), \(1/\infty=0\). Miller indices: \((100)\).

    Medium

    Problem 9

    Show explicitly that the body-centered cubic structure contains 2 atoms per conventional unit cell.

    Show Solution

    8 corner atoms contribute \(8\times\tfrac18=1\) atom, plus 1 whole atom at the body center, entirely inside the cell. Total: \(1+1=2\) atoms per cell.

    Problem 10

    Derive the touching condition (relating atomic radius \(r\) to lattice constant \(a\)) for the body-centered cubic structure.

    Show Solution

    In BCC, the corner atoms touch the body-center atom along the cube's body diagonal, which has length \(\sqrt3\,a\). That diagonal spans a radius from each corner atom plus the full diameter of the body-center atom, four atomic radii in total: \(4r = \sqrt3\,a\).

    Problem 11

    Germanium crystallizes in the diamond cubic structure with lattice constant \(a=0.566\) nm. Find its nearest-neighbor (bond) distance.

    Show Solution

    \(d = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{4}a = \dfrac{1.732}{4}(0.566\ \text{nm}) = 0.245\ \text{nm}\).

    Problem 12

    A crystal plane intercepts the \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) axes at \(1a\), \(1a\), and \(\infty\), respectively. Find its Miller indices.

    Show Solution

    Reciprocals: \(1,\ 1,\ 0\). Already integers, so the Miller indices are \((110)\) — a plane parallel to the \(z\) axis.

    Problem 13

    A crystal plane has Miller indices \((200)\). Reduce these to the standard smallest-integer form and describe the plane's orientation.

    Show Solution

    Miller indices are always reduced to the smallest integers sharing no common factor, so \((200)\) reduces to \((100)\). This describes a plane parallel to the \(y\)-\(z\) face of the cube, the same family of planes commonly used to cut silicon wafers.

    Problem 14

    Explain why silicon wafers are commonly cut along the \((100)\) or \((111)\) planes rather than an arbitrary orientation.

    Show Solution

    Crystal planes differ in atomic density and bonding, so cutting along specific low-index planes such as \((100)\) or \((111)\) gives predictable, low-defect surfaces well suited to growing oxide layers and other fabrication steps, and lets manufacturers exploit the crystal's natural cleavage behavior when dicing wafers.

    Problem 15

    A metal crystallizes in the FCC structure with atomic radius \(r = 0.128\) nm. Find its lattice constant.

    Show Solution

    From the FCC touching condition \(4r=\sqrt2\,a\): \(a = \dfrac{4r}{\sqrt2} = \dfrac{4(0.128\ \text{nm})}{1.414} = 0.362\ \text{nm}\).

    Problem 16

    Explain why the zincblende structure lacks the inversion symmetry that the diamond structure has.

    Show Solution

    In diamond, every atom is chemically identical, so inverting the crystal through any atom maps it exactly onto itself. In zincblende, the two interpenetrating sublattices hold two different atomic species, so inverting through one species would map its neighbors onto positions occupied by the other species — the crystal is not invariant under inversion, since the two species are not interchangeable.

    Difficult

    Problem 17

    Derive the packing fraction of the body-centered cubic structure from its touching condition.

    Show Solution

    The touching condition is \(4r=\sqrt3\,a\), so \(r=\sqrt3\,a/4\). With 2 atoms per cell, each of volume \(\tfrac43\pi r^3\): packing fraction \(=\dfrac{2\times\tfrac43\pi r^3}{a^3} = \dfrac{\tfrac83\pi\left(\dfrac{\sqrt3\,a}{4}\right)^3}{a^3} = \dfrac{\tfrac83\pi\cdot\dfrac{3\sqrt3}{64}a^3}{a^3} = \dfrac{\sqrt3\,\pi}{8}\approx 0.680\).

    Problem 18

    Gallium arsenide (GaAs) crystallizes in the zincblende structure with lattice constant \(a=0.565\) nm. Estimate its mass density, given that the average atomic mass of the two-atom Ga-As basis is 72.3 u (the average of Ga at 69.7 u and As at 74.9 u), and there are 8 atoms per conventional cell. Use \(N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}\).

    Show Solution

    Cell volume: \(a^3 = (0.565\times10^{-7}\ \text{cm})^3 = 1.804\times10^{-22}\ \text{cm}^3\). Mass of 8 atoms at average mass 72.3 u: \(m = 8\times\dfrac{72.3\ \text{g/mol}}{6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}} = 8\times1.201\times10^{-22}\ \text{g} = 9.60\times10^{-22}\ \text{g}\). Density: \(\rho = m/a^3 = (9.60\times10^{-22})/(1.804\times10^{-22}) = 5.32\ \text{g/cm}^3\), close to the accepted value for GaAs of about 5.32 g/cm\(^3\).

    Problem 19

    A crystal plane intercepts the \(x\) axis at \(3a\), the \(y\) axis at \(2a\), and the \(z\) axis at \(1a\). Find its Miller indices, showing every step.

    Show Solution

    Intercepts (in units of \(a\)): \(3,\ 2,\ 1\). Reciprocals: \(1/3,\ 1/2,\ 1/1\). Clearing fractions by multiplying through by the least common denominator, 6: \(6/3=2,\ 6/2=3,\ 6/1=6\). Miller indices: \((236)\).

    Problem 20

    Two crystals have the same atomic radius \(r\). One is simple cubic and the other is face-centered cubic. Find the ratio of their lattice constants, \(a_{\text{FCC}}/a_{\text{SC}}\).

    Show Solution

    From the touching conditions: \(a_{\text{SC}}=2r\) and \(a_{\text{FCC}}=4r/\sqrt2 = 2\sqrt2\,r\). Ratio: \(a_{\text{FCC}}/a_{\text{SC}} = 2\sqrt2\,r/(2r) = \sqrt2 \approx 1.414\). The FCC cell is larger, but it also contains 4 atoms rather than 1, which is why it still achieves a higher packing fraction.

    Problem 21

    Silicon has lattice constant \(a=0.543\) nm and diamond cubic structure (8 atoms/cell). Using its actual atomic mass of 28.09 u, verify the accepted silicon density of 2.33 g/cm\(^3\) to within 1%, and then estimate what its density would be if it crystallized instead in a simple cubic structure with the same lattice constant.

    Show Solution

    Diamond cubic: cell volume \(a^3=1.601\times10^{-22}\ \text{cm}^3\), mass of 8 atoms \(=8\times(28.09/6.022\times10^{23})=3.732\times10^{-22}\ \text{g}\), giving \(\rho=3.732\times10^{-22}/1.601\times10^{-22}=2.33\ \text{g/cm}^3\), matching the accepted value within rounding. If the same lattice constant instead described a simple cubic structure with only 1 atom per cell, the mass would be 8 times smaller while the cell volume stays the same, so the density would be \(2.33/8 \approx 0.29\ \text{g/cm}^3\) — dramatically lower, illustrating how strongly the atoms-per-cell count (and hence the specific crystal structure) determines a material's density at a fixed lattice constant.
