What to Observe

  • Predict: Before switching to "n-type (electrons)" with the same current and field, predict whether the Hall voltage sign will flip.
  • Observe: Switch carrier type and confirm the Hall voltage sign reverses, even though current and field directions are unchanged — this sign flip is exactly how the Hall effect identifies carrier type experimentally.
  • Try this: Reverse the magnetic field slider's sign and watch the accumulation edges swap.
  • Compare: Switch between "Heavily doped" and "Lightly doped" presets and compare the resulting Hall voltage magnitude.

Connects to Chapter 12: In the Hall effect, a magnetic field perpendicular to a current-carrying bar deflects moving carriers sideways via the Lorentz force, building up charge on one edge until the resulting transverse electric field balances the magnetic deflection. The resulting Hall voltage is \(V_H=R_H IB/t\), where the Hall coefficient \(R_H=1/(qp)\) for holes or \(R_H=-1/(qn)\) for electrons — its sign directly reveals whether the majority carrier is a hole or an electron, one of the few direct experimental probes of carrier type and concentration.

Challenge Questions

1. If the measured Hall voltage on a sample is negative (with the field and current directions as shown), what does that reveal about the sample?

The Hall coefficient's sign directly reflects the majority carrier's charge sign; a negative Hall voltage (with this sim's conventions) indicates electron (n-type) conduction.

Switch the carrier type dropdown and watch which one produces a negative computed Hall voltage.

2. What physically causes the Hall voltage to build up?

The Lorentz force pushes moving carriers toward one edge; the resulting charge buildup creates a transverse electric field that grows until it exactly cancels the magnetic deflection force, at which point a steady Hall voltage exists.

Recall the Lorentz force law, F=qv×B, and think about what happens to a moving charge in a perpendicular magnetic field.

3. Predict first: does a more heavily doped sample produce a larger or smaller Hall voltage magnitude, at the same current and field?

R_H is inversely proportional to carrier concentration, so heavier doping gives a smaller Hall coefficient and a smaller Hall voltage for the same current and field — compare the "Heavily doped" and "Lightly doped" presets to confirm.

Switch between the "Heavily doped (weak signal)" and "Lightly doped (strong signal)" presets and compare V_H.

4. The readout shows R_H = −6.24×10⁻³ m³/C for a sample. What does the negative sign tell you?

R_H = 1/(qp) is positive for holes and R_H = −1/(qn) is negative for electrons, so a negative R_H directly identifies electron (n-type) conduction — independent of the chosen B or I direction, which is why R_H, not just V_H, is the quantity reported in materials tables.

Compare the R_H formulas for holes and electrons in the "Connects to Chapter 12" text above, and watch how the readout's sign flips when you switch the carrier type dropdown.

Key Takeaway: The Hall effect measures something equilibrium carrier-concentration equations alone cannot: the actual sign of the majority carrier's charge. It is also a practical sensitivity trade-off — lightly-doped samples give a larger, easier-to-measure Hall voltage, which is why real Hall sensors are deliberately lightly doped.
