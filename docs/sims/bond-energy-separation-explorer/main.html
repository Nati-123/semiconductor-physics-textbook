Try This

  • Try this: Drag the "Current separation r" slider from large r down toward r0 and watch the marker slide down the purple curve into the well, then past r0 where it climbs the steep repulsive wall.
  • Predict: Before dragging r below r0, predict whether the two-atom diagram will show inward or outward arrows once r is smaller than r0.
  • Observe: Press "Snap r to r0" and confirm the readout reports "Equilibrium — net force ≈ 0" and U(r) equals exactly −E0.
  • Compare: Click "Covalent Si–Si" and then "Ionic Na–Cl" and compare both the equilibrium separation and the depth of the energy well.
  • Explain: Explain, using the blue (attractive) and red (repulsive) dashed curves, why the total energy curve is flat and near zero at large r but rises steeply at small r.

Connects to Chapter 4: This MicroSim supplies the missing link between "atoms bond" and "why a stable bond distance exists." The attractive term (dashed blue) is the same long-range Coulomb-like attraction discussed in the ionic bonding section — compare the "Ionic Na–Cl" preset's bond energy, 5.1 eV, to this chapter's Worked Example 3, which computes \(U(r) = -e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r) \approx -5.1\text{ eV}\) at \(r = 0.28\) nm using that same Coulomb formula. The repulsive term (dashed red) represents the short-range overlap of filled electron shells, which grows extremely fast at small r. Their sum has a single minimum at the equilibrium separation \(r_0\) with depth \(E_0\), the bond energy — exactly the two numbers this chapter's bonding discussion treats as given.

What Each Control Does

Equilibrium separation r0 slider
Sets where the total energy curve's minimum sits, in nanometers — this is the bond length.
Bond energy E0 slider
Sets how deep the energy well is, in eV — this is the energy needed to pull the bond apart.
Current separation r slider
Moves the black marker along the total energy curve and updates the two-atom diagram below the graph.
Snap r to r0 button
Jumps the current separation directly to the equilibrium value.
Covalent Si–Si / Ionic Na–Cl preset buttons
Load realistic r0 and E0 values for the two bond types discussed in this chapter.

Challenge Questions

1. What is U(r) when the current separation r equals r0 exactly?

The curve is built so its minimum value is exactly −E0 at r = r0 — press "Snap r to r0" and read U(r) in the readout panel to confirm.

Press "Snap r to r0" and read the U(r) value reported in the readout panel.

2. If r is smaller than r0, what does the readout panel report?

Pushing the atoms closer than r0 makes the short-range repulsive term dominate, so the atoms push apart back toward r0.

Drag the "Current separation r" slider to a value below r0 and read the colored state line in the readout panel.

3. Which curve represents the short-range repulsive term?

The legend below the graph marks the red dashed curve as the repulsive term — it stays near zero at large r and shoots upward only when atoms are pushed very close together.

Check the legend row in the readout panel below the graph, which color-codes each curve.

4. Comparing the "Covalent Si–Si" and "Ionic Na–Cl" presets, how does the Na–Cl bond energy compare to Chapter 4's Worked Example 3?

The Ionic Na–Cl preset uses r0 = 0.28 nm and E0 = 5.1 eV, the same numbers Chapter 4's Worked Example 3 computes directly from \(U(r) = -e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\).

Click "Ionic Na–Cl" and compare the r0 and E0 slider values to the chapter's Worked Example 3.
