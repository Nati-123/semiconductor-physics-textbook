Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

Try This

  • Try this: Set ψs to exactly 0 and confirm the semiconductor bands are perfectly flat from the oxide interface into the bulk — the flat-band condition.
  • Predict: Before raising ψs above 2φF, predict which band (EC or EV) will end up closest to EF right at the surface.
  • Observe: The dashed EF line stays fixed while EC and EV move — band bending changes the bands' position relative to a fixed Fermi level, exactly as in the p-n junction depletion region.

Connects to Chapter 16: A MOS capacitor stacks a gate, an insulating gate oxide, and a semiconductor. The flat-band voltage is the gate bias at which the semiconductor bands show no bending at all; moving away from it bends the bands at the semiconductor surface, quantified by the surface potential \(\psi_s\).

What Each Control Does

ψs
Surface potential, standing in qualitatively for gate bias relative to flat-band; positive values bend bands down at the surface (toward inversion for a p-type substrate).
NA
Substrate doping, which sets φF and therefore the weak- and strong-inversion thresholds.

Challenge Questions

1. At exactly ψs = 0, what is the state of the semiconductor bands?

Correct. ψs = 0 is defined as the flat-band condition, where the semiconductor bands show no bending all the way to the surface.

Recall how ψs was defined in the chapter — what value corresponds to the flat-band voltage?

2. What does the oxide region in this diagram represent physically?

Correct. The gate oxide is an excellent insulator (SiO2), so essentially no current flows through it — but it does transmit the electric field, which is how the gate controls the semiconductor surface electrostatically.

Recall why a MOS capacitor can hold a gate voltage indefinitely without driving current through the structure.
