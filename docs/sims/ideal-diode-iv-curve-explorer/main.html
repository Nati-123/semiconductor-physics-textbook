Try This

  • Try this: Switch to Semi-Log View and watch the forward region collapse into a straight line.
  • Try this: Click "Compare 300K vs T2" and drag the T2 slider well above and below 300 K to see how much the curve shifts.
  • Predict: Before raising temperature T, predict whether the forward curve at a fixed voltage will shift to higher or lower current.
  • Observe: Dragging the voltage marker into reverse bias (negative V) shows J leveling off at −J0 almost immediately, well before reaching -2 V — this is the shaded "Reverse Saturation" band.
  • Observe: As the marker crosses into the green "Exponential Forward Conduction" band, the region badge below the voltage readout changes color and label to match.

Connects to Chapter 15: The ideal diode equation, \(J=J_0(e^{V/V_T}-1)\), combines the law of the junction with the saturation current \(J_0\) derived from the injected minority carrier profile's gradient. It captures the diode's entire behavior: zero current at equilibrium, exponential forward conduction, and small, nearly voltage-independent reverse current. Because current depends on voltage through an exponential, each additional \(V_T\) of forward bias multiplies the current by a constant factor \(e\) — this is why the forward current increases exponentially rather than linearly, and why it appears as a straight line only on the semi-log view.

What Each Control Does

J0
Sets the saturation current density (log scale), shifting the whole curve up or down.
T
In Single Temperature mode, sets the curve's temperature, which sets the thermal voltage VT and therefore the forward curve's steepness.
Single Temperature / Compare 300K vs T2
Toggles a second curve, in orange, at an independently-adjustable second temperature T2, plotted alongside the primary curve fixed at 300 K.
T2
(Compare mode only) Sets the second curve's temperature, so its VT and steepness can be compared directly against the 300 K curve.
View
Switches between linear and semi-log (log₁₀|J|) display.
V marker
Reads off J at a chosen voltage and shows which labeled region (Reverse Saturation, Near-Zero Bias, or Exponential Forward Conduction) that voltage falls in.

Challenge Questions

1. In the semi-log view, what does the forward region of the I-V curve look like?

Correct. Since J grows exponentially with V, log(J) grows linearly with V, producing a straight line on the semi-log plot.

Recall that a semi-log plot turns exponential growth into a straight line — that's exactly why it's used here.

2. What value does J approach under reverse bias, well before breakdown?

Correct. Once V is a few VT negative, e^(V/VT) is essentially zero, so J approaches the small, fixed value −J0 — the reverse "saturation" current.

Set V to a large negative value on the marker and read off where J levels out.

3. Why does the forward current increase exponentially with voltage rather than, say, linearly?

Correct. The ideal diode equation's exponential term e^(V/VT) means every additional VT of forward bias multiplies the current by the same factor, e ≈ 2.718 — the hallmark of exponential growth, not linear growth.

Look at the equation itself: V appears in an exponent, e^(V/VT). Equal increments of V in that exponent produce equal ratios of J, not equal differences — that's exponential behavior.

4. In Compare mode, raising T2 well above 300 K mainly does what to the orange curve's forward region?

Correct. A higher T2 increases VT = kT/q, which stretches out the exponential — the curve needs more volts to reach the same current, so it looks less steep next to the 300 K curve.

Watch the two curves' forward "knees" as you drag T2 up: the higher-T2 curve's knee moves to a higher voltage because VT = kT/q grows with T.
