### Problem 22

A MOS capacitor has a p+ polysilicon gate (\(\Phi_M=5.17\ \text{V}\)), \(N_A=8\times10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), and \(t_{ox}=6\ \text{nm}\). Find \(V_T\).

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;"><span class="arithmatex">\(\phi_F=0.0259\ln(8\times10^{16}/1.5\times10^{10})\approx0.401\ \text{V}\)</span>. <span class="arithmatex">\(\Phi_S\approx5.044\ \text{V}\)</span>, so <span class="arithmatex">\(V_{FB}=5.17-5.044\approx0.126\ \text{V}\)</span>. <span class="arithmatex">\(C_{ox}=(3.9)(8.85\times10^{-14})/(6\times10^{-7})\approx5.75\times10^{-7}\ \text{F/cm}^2\)</span>. <span class="arithmatex">\(Q_{dep,max}=\sqrt{4(1.035\times10^{-12})(1.6\times10^{-19})(8\times10^{16})(0.401)}\approx1.459\times10^{-7}\ \text{C/cm}^2\)</span>, so \(Q_{dep,max}/C_{ox}\approx0.254\ \text{V}\). <span class="arithmatex">\(V_T\approx0.126+0.802+0.254\approx1.182\ \text{V}\)</span> — a healthy, comfortably positive threshold, unlike the marginal aluminum-gate example in the chapter text, illustrating why heavily-doped p+ polysilicon gates were historically preferred for well-controlled NMOS threshold voltages.</p>

### Problem 23

Two otherwise-identical MOS capacitors (aluminum gate, \(N_A=1\times10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\)) differ only in oxide thickness: one has \(t_{ox}=25\ \text{nm}\), the other \(t_{ox}=5\ \text{nm}\). Find \(V_T\) for both and comment on the difference.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Both capacitors share \(\phi_F\approx0.347\ \text{V}\), \(V_{FB}\approx-0.890\ \text{V}\), and \(Q_{dep,max}\approx4.80\times10^{-8}\ \text{C/cm}^2\) (none of these depend on \(t_{ox}\)). At \(t_{ox}=25\ \text{nm}\): \(C_{ox}\approx1.381\times10^{-7}\ \text{F/cm}^2\), giving \(V_T\approx0.152\ \text{V}\). At \(t_{ox}=5\ \text{nm}\): \(C_{ox}\approx6.903\times10^{-7}\ \text{F/cm}^2\), giving \(V_T\approx-0.126\ \text{V}\). The five-fold thinner oxide pulled \(V_T\) down by nearly 0.28 V and flipped its sign — oxide thickness alone can be the difference between a workable and an unusable threshold voltage.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 24

Real integrated circuits achieve ohmic contacts almost universally by heavily doping the semiconductor right at the contact (\(N>10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\)), rather than relying on choosing a metal whose work function happens to align favorably. Explain why this approach is more practical.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Relying on work-function alignment requires choosing a specific metal for every doping type and concentration used on a chip — impractical when a single process may need ohmic contacts to both n-type and p-type regions, often using the same metal for manufacturing simplicity. Heavy doping instead makes the Schottky barrier's depletion width so thin (recall \(W\propto1/\sqrt{N}\)) that carriers tunnel straight through it regardless of the nominal barrier height, giving a low-resistance contact almost independent of which metal is used — a single metallization scheme can then form good ohmic contacts to both n+ and p+ regions on the same chip.</p>
</div>
</details>
