Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

Try This

  • Try this: With temperature set low (near 100 K), click Start and watch how rarely a covalent bond breaks.
  • Predict: Before dragging the temperature slider to 900 K, predict whether the number of broken bonds will grow a little or a lot.
  • Observe: Drag the temperature slider up while the animation runs and watch the broken-bond count rise sharply — each broken bond is one thermally generated electron-hole pair.
  • Explain: Explain, in your own words, why every broken bond in this diagram creates exactly one free electron and exactly one hole, never just one or the other.

Connects to Chapter 7: Every silicon atom shares one covalent bond (an electron pair) with each of its four neighbors. In this simplified 2D schematic, a bond breaking represents a valence electron gaining enough thermal energy to leave its bond, becoming a free electron, and leaving behind a hole. This process — intrinsic (thermal) generation of electron-hole pairs in a pure semiconductor — is what defines an intrinsic semiconductor. The exact equation for how strongly carrier concentration depends on temperature is derived in Chapter 9; here, the qualitative trend (more broken bonds at higher T) is illustrated directly.

What Each Control Does

Temperature slider
Sets the lattice temperature from 100 K to 900 K, controlling how often a bond thermally breaks.
Start/Stop button
Starts or pauses the bond-breaking and recombination animation. The sim loads paused so you can inspect the intact lattice first.
Reset button
Restores the default temperature and clears all broken bonds.

Challenge Questions

1. In this MicroSim, what does a single broken bond represent physically?

Breaking a covalent bond frees one valence electron while leaving behind exactly one hole in the same location — the two are always created together in an intrinsic semiconductor.

Look at what appears at a broken bond: both a blue electron marker and a red hole marker.

2. Why does the number of broken bonds increase so sharply as temperature rises?

Thermal bond-breaking follows an exponential (Boltzmann-like) dependence on temperature, so even modest temperature increases produce large increases in the number of broken bonds.

Compare the broken-bond count at 100 K versus 900 K after letting the animation run for a few seconds at each.
