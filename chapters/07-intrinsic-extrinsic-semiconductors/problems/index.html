    End-of-Chapter Problems: Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

    Work through these problems to reinforce your understanding of intrinsic/extrinsic material, elemental/compound semiconductors, and donor/acceptor doping covered in Chapter 7.

    Easy

    Problem 1

    Define, in one sentence, an intrinsic semiconductor.

    Show Solution

    A chemically pure semiconductor whose only carriers come from thermally-broken covalent bonds, each producing one free electron and one hole together.

    Problem 2

    What is the key structural difference between an elemental and a compound semiconductor?

    Show Solution

    An elemental semiconductor is built from a single atomic species (e.g. silicon); a compound semiconductor alternates two different species (e.g. gallium and arsenic in GaAs).

    Problem 3

    How many valence electrons does a Group V donor atom contribute to covalent bonds, and how many are left over?

    Show Solution

    4 electrons fill covalent bonds (same as a Group IV host atom); the 5th electron is left over, only weakly bound.

    Problem 4

    How many valence electrons does a Group III acceptor atom contribute to covalent bonds, and what is left incomplete?

    Show Solution

    3 electrons complete 3 covalent bonds; the 4th bond is left incomplete, forming a hole.

    Problem 5

    Is silicon direct-gap or indirect-gap? What about gallium arsenide?

    Show Solution

    Silicon is indirect-gap; gallium arsenide is direct-gap.

    Problem 6

    State the Varshni equation.

    Show Solution

    \(E_g(T) = E_g(0) - \dfrac{\alpha T^2}{T+\beta}\).

    Problem 7

    What sign of fixed charge is left behind by an ionized donor atom? By an ionized acceptor atom?

    Show Solution

    An ionized donor becomes a fixed positive ion; an ionized acceptor becomes a fixed negative ion.

    Problem 8

    State the hydrogenic donor ionization energy formula.

    Show Solution

    \(E_D \approx 13.6\ \text{eV}\times\dfrac{m^*/m_0}{\varepsilon_r^2}\).

    Medium

    Problem 9

    Explain why a broken covalent bond in an intrinsic semiconductor always produces exactly one electron and one hole, never one without the other.

    Show Solution

    A broken bond and its resulting vacancy are two descriptions of the same single event: the freed electron becomes the free carrier, and the vacancy it leaves behind is the hole. There is no separate mechanism that could create one without the other in a pure crystal.

    Problem 10

    Indium arsenide (InAs) is a compound semiconductor. Using periodic table group numbers, explain why In and As can pair to form a stable semiconductor.

    Show Solution

    Indium is Group III (3 valence electrons) and arsenic is Group V (5 valence electrons); paired together they average 4 valence electrons per atom, matching silicon's covalent-bonding count and allowing the same zincblende bonding geometry as GaAs.

    Problem 11

    Using silicon's Varshni constants (\(E_g(0)=1.166\) eV, \(\alpha=4.73\times10^{-4}\) eV/K, \(\beta=636\) K), find silicon's band gap at \(T=100\) K.

    Show Solution

    \(E_g(100) = 1.166 - \dfrac{(4.73\times10^{-4})(100)^2}{100+636} = 1.166 - \dfrac{4.73}{736} = 1.166-0.0064 \approx 1.160\ \text{eV}\). At low temperature the gap is very close to its \(T=0\) extrapolated value.

    Problem 12

    A silicon sample is doped with boron at \(N_A=5\times10^{15}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). Using \(N_{Si}\approx5\times10^{22}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), find the ratio of silicon atoms to acceptor atoms.

    Show Solution

    \(N_{Si}/N_A = (5\times10^{22})/(5\times10^{15}) = 10^7\) — about 10 million silicon atoms per boron atom.

    Problem 13

    Explain why GaAs requires more careful stoichiometric control during crystal growth than silicon does.

    Show Solution

    Silicon has only one atomic species, so there is no ratio to control. GaAs must be grown with gallium and arsenic in very nearly a 1:1 ratio; deviations introduce antisite defects (a Ga atom on an As site, or vice versa) that disrupt bonding and degrade performance.

    Problem 14

    Using \(m^*/m_0=0.12\) and \(\varepsilon_r=16.2\) (germanium's approximate values), estimate the hydrogenic donor ionization energy for germanium.

    Show Solution

    \(E_D \approx 13.6\times\dfrac{0.12}{16.2^2} = 13.6\times\dfrac{0.12}{262.4} \approx 13.6\times4.57\times10^{-4} \approx 0.0062\ \text{eV} = 6.2\ \text{meV}\), the right order of magnitude compared to germanium's real measured donor ionization energies (roughly 10-13 meV).

    Problem 15

    Why does silicon dominate digital integrated circuits despite germanium having higher carrier mobility?

    Show Solution

    Silicon's much higher melting point gives it a wider thermal processing window for high-temperature fabrication steps, and its excellent native oxide (SiO2) is essential for building high-quality MOSFETs — advantages that outweigh germanium's mobility edge for mainstream digital manufacturing.

    Problem 16

    At room temperature, is a typical donor or acceptor atom in silicon mostly ionized or mostly un-ionized? Justify your answer using the ionization energies discussed in this chapter.

    Show Solution

    Mostly ionized. Measured donor/acceptor ionization energies in silicon (tens of meV) are comparable to or only somewhat larger than room-temperature thermal energy (\(k_BT\approx26\) meV), so most dopant atoms have enough thermal energy to ionize — the basis of the "complete ionization" assumption used starting in Chapter 8.

    Difficult

    Problem 17

    A hypothetical semiconductor has intrinsic carrier concentration \(n_i=2\times10^{9}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) at room temperature and is doped with a donor concentration \(N_D=3\times10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). By what factor does doping increase the free carrier concentration compared to the intrinsic case (assuming complete ionization and that donor electrons dominate)?

    Show Solution

    \(N_D/n_i = (3\times10^{16})/(2\times10^{9}) = 1.5\times10^{7}\) — doping increases the carrier concentration by about 15 million times, illustrating why extrinsic behavior overwhelms intrinsic behavior at any practical doping level.

    Problem 18

    Explain, using the hydrogenic model, why a material with a very large dielectric constant but only a modestly reduced effective mass would still be expected to have a very small dopant ionization energy.

    Show Solution

    Because \(E_D\propto(m^*/m_0)/\varepsilon_r^2\), the dielectric constant appears squared in the denominator, so it dominates the result far more strongly than a modest change in effective mass would. A large \(\varepsilon_r\) screens the Coulomb attraction so effectively that ionization energy drops sharply, even if the effective mass reduction alone is modest.

    Problem 19

    A crystal grower produces a GaAs sample that is slightly As-rich rather than perfectly stoichiometric. Predict, qualitatively, what kind of defect this introduces and why this is a concern unique to compound (rather than elemental) semiconductors.

    Show Solution

    An As-rich sample has more arsenic atoms than needed for perfect 1:1 stoichiometry, so some excess As atoms must occupy sites that should hold Ga — an antisite defect (As on a Ga site). Elemental semiconductors like silicon have only one atomic species, so there is no stoichiometric ratio to violate in the first place; this is a concern unique to compound semiconductors.

    Problem 20

    A silicon sample must be doped so that dopant atoms are no more than 1 in 10 million silicon atoms. Using \(N_{Si}\approx5\times10^{22}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), find the maximum allowed doping concentration, and identify which of this chapter's earlier worked examples used a doping level in this same range.

    Show Solution

    Maximum \(N_D = N_{Si}/10^7 = (5\times10^{22})/10^7 = 5\times10^{15}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). This matches the boron doping level used in the earlier acceptor-ratio worked example, which also produced a ratio of about 10 million silicon atoms per dopant atom.
