Connects to Chapter 12: In the Hall effect, a magnetic field perpendicular to a current-carrying bar deflects moving carriers sideways via the Lorentz force, building up charge on one edge until the resulting transverse electric field balances the magnetic deflection. The resulting Hall voltage is \(V_H=R_H IB/t\), where the Hall coefficient \(R_H=1/(qp)\) for holes or \(R_H=-1/(qn)\) for electrons — its sign directly reveals whether the majority carrier is a hole or an electron, one of the few direct experimental probes of carrier type and concentration.

Challenge Questions

1. If the measured Hall voltage on a sample is negative (with the field and current directions as shown), what does that reveal about the sample?

The Hall coefficient's sign directly reflects the majority carrier's charge sign; a negative Hall voltage (with this sim's conventions) indicates electron (n-type) conduction.

Switch the carrier type dropdown and watch which one produces a negative computed Hall voltage.

2. What physically causes the Hall voltage to build up?

The Lorentz force pushes moving carriers toward one edge; the resulting charge buildup creates a transverse electric field that grows until it exactly cancels the magnetic deflection force, at which point a steady Hall voltage exists.

Recall the Lorentz force law, F=qv×B, and think about what happens to a moving charge in a perpendicular magnetic field.
