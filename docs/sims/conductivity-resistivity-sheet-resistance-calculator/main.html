Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

What to Observe

  • The card reports every quantity in the chain — μ, σ, ρ, R_s, R — computed live in sequence from your slider settings, not looked up from a table.
  • The resistor diagram is drawn purely from the "squares" count; its absolute size on screen is just for illustration and never affects the reported resistance.
  • Presets jump directly to representative design points, from lightly-doped material to a heavily-doped IC contact to a long, narrow resistor layout.

Guided Exploration

  • Try this: Raise the doping concentration and watch resistivity fall, even though mobility itself is dropping (due to impurity scattering) — carrier concentration wins out.
  • Predict: Before changing the number-of-squares slider, predict whether resistance depends on the resistor's absolute size or only its length-to-width ratio.
  • Observe: Change the squares slider and watch total resistance scale directly with the number of squares, regardless of the film's absolute dimensions.
  • Compare: Switch between the "Lightly doped" and "Heavily doped IC contact" presets and compare how many orders of magnitude R_s changes.

Connects to Chapter 11: Conductivity, \(\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)\), combines carrier concentration and mobility into the single number that governs how well a doped semiconductor conducts. For a thin film (as in integrated circuit resistors), sheet resistance, \(R_s=\rho/t\) (ohms per square), is more useful than resistivity alone, since a rectangular resistor's total resistance is simply \(R=R_s\times(L/W)\) — the number of "squares" the film is divided into, independent of its absolute size.

What Each Control Does

Preset dropdown
Jumps directly to representative design points; switches to "Custom" once you move a slider.
Carrier type dropdown
Selects electron or hole conduction, with its own mobility model.
Doping concentration slider
Sets carrier concentration, computing mobility via Matthiessen's rule at 300 K.
Film thickness slider
Sets the film thickness used to convert resistivity into sheet resistance.
Number of squares (L/W) slider
Sets the resistor's length-to-width ratio, drawn as a grid of squares.

Challenge Questions

1. Why does resistivity generally decrease as doping concentration increases, even though mobility also decreases?

Conductivity is the product of carrier concentration and mobility; over most practical doping ranges, the large increase in carrier concentration outweighs the more modest decrease in mobility from added impurity scattering, so conductivity rises and resistivity falls.

Compare how many orders of magnitude the doping slider spans versus how much the computed mobility actually changes.

2. Two resistors are made from the same film but one is twice as long and twice as wide as the other. How do their resistances compare?

Since R = Rs×(L/W), and doubling both L and W leaves the ratio L/W unchanged, the two resistors have identical resistance despite their different absolute sizes.

Compute L/W for "2L, 2W" versus "L, W" and compare.

3. Why does a thinner film have a higher sheet resistance at the same doping level?

R_s=ρ/t is inversely proportional to thickness — halving the thickness with resistivity unchanged doubles R_s. Try the "Heavily doped IC contact" preset's thin film and compare R_s to the "Typical extrinsic" preset at the same doping trend.

Drag the thickness slider down while holding doping fixed and watch R_s in the card.

Key Takeaway: σ, ρ, and R_s are not independent quantities to memorize separately — they are the same underlying nμ product, viewed as a conductance, its reciprocal, and its per-unit-thickness form. Once you know doping, carrier type, and film thickness, the entire chain — μ, σ, ρ, R_s, and R for any layout — follows automatically.
