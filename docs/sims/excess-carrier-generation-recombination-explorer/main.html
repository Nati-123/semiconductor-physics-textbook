Try This

  • Try this: Drag the time marker slowly from left to right and watch Δn rise toward Δn_ss during generation, then decay after the dashed "generation stops" line.
  • Predict: Before increasing τ, predict whether the decay curve will fall more quickly or more slowly.
  • Observe: A longer minority carrier lifetime τ makes both the rise and the decay noticeably more gradual, since τ sets the natural timescale of both phases.

Connects to Chapter 13: Excess carriers are carriers above the equilibrium concentration, created by carrier generation (optical or thermal) and removed by carrier recombination. Once generation stops, excess carriers decay exponentially with time constant equal to the minority carrier lifetime τ, and the instantaneous recombination rate is simply \(R=\Delta n/\tau\).

What Each Control Does

Generation source
Labels whether the excess carriers pictured come from optical (light pulse) or thermal (background) generation — both feed the same Δn(t) curve physically.
Δn_ss exponent
Sets the steady-state excess carrier concentration reached while generation is active.
τ (lifetime)
Sets the minority carrier lifetime controlling both the rise and decay timescales.
Time marker
Scrubs through the rise and decay phases, reading off Δn and the instantaneous recombination rate.

Challenge Questions

1. After generation stops, what does the excess carrier concentration do?

Once generation stops, recombination alone governs the excess carriers, producing an exponential decay Δn(t)=Δn_ss·exp(-t/τ).

Watch the red curve after the dashed "generation stops" line — it falls off smoothly, not instantly or linearly.
