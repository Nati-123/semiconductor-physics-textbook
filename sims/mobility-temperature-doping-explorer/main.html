Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

What to Observe

  • Three curves per carrier: green (lattice-limited μ_L, falling with T), orange (impurity-limited μ_I, rising with T), and a third color (total μ, always the lowest of the two — never higher than either individual mechanism).
  • The "Compare electrons vs. holes" checkbox overlays both carrier types at once (holes dashed), making the electron/hole mobility gap directly visible.
  • The 77 K / 300 K / 500 K buttons jump the temperature marker to standard reference points instantly.

Guided Exploration

  • Try this: Set doping to its lowest value and observe that the total mobility curve nearly follows the lattice-scattering curve alone.
  • Predict: Before raising the doping concentration, predict which curve (lattice or impurity) will start to dominate at low temperature.
  • Observe: Raise doping and watch the impurity-scattering curve drop and pull the total mobility curve down with it, especially at low temperature.
  • Compare: Enable "Compare electrons vs. holes" and confirm the electron curve sits above the hole curve at every temperature — electrons are consistently more mobile in silicon.

Connects to Chapter 11: Two independent scattering mechanisms limit carrier mobility: lattice scattering off thermal vibrations of the crystal (which gets worse — mobility drops — as temperature rises, roughly \(\mu_L\propto T^{-3/2}\)), and impurity scattering off ionized dopant atoms (which gets worse at low temperature, where carriers move slowly enough to be deflected by the impurities' Coulomb field, roughly \(\mu_I\propto T^{3/2}/N\)). Matthiessen's rule combines the two independently-acting scattering rates: \(1/\mu_{total}=1/\mu_L+1/\mu_I\), so whichever mechanism gives the *smaller* individual mobility dominates the total.

What Each Control Does

Carrier type dropdown
Switches between electron and hole mobility parameters (ignored while comparison mode is on).
Compare electrons vs. holes checkbox
Overlays both carrier types' total-mobility curves together instead of showing one at a time.
Doping concentration slider
Sets the ionized impurity concentration, controlling the impurity-scattering curve.
Temperature marker slider / 77 K, 300 K, 500 K presets
Reads off all three mobility values at a chosen temperature.

Challenge Questions

1. According to Matthiessen's rule, if impurity scattering gives a much smaller mobility than lattice scattering, what dominates the total mobility?

Because Matthiessen's rule adds reciprocal mobilities (like resistors in parallel), the mechanism with the smaller individual mobility — the more effective scatterer — dominates the total.

Try setting doping very high and low temperature, then compare the impurity curve to the total curve.

2. Why does lattice-scattering-limited mobility decrease as temperature increases?

Higher temperature means more vigorous lattice vibrations (phonons), which scatter carriers more often, reducing lattice-limited mobility.

Look at the lattice-scattering curve's slope as the temperature marker moves right.

3. In the comparison mode, why do electrons and holes have different total-mobility curves even at the same temperature and doping?

Electrons and holes move through the same lattice and the same impurity atoms, but their different effective masses and band structures give them different scattering strengths, hence different μ_L0/μ_I0 reference values and different curves.

Enable comparison mode and look at the two "Total μ" curves' vertical separation.

Key Takeaway: Mobility is not one fixed material constant — it is the outcome of two competing, temperature- and doping-dependent scattering mechanisms combined by Matthiessen's rule, and it differs between electrons and holes in the same crystal because each carrier's effective mass and scattering strength differ.
