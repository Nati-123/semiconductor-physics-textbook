  • Predict: Before setting Eg to silicon's 1.12 eV, predict whether the emission would be visible.
  • Observe: Only a fairly narrow band-gap window (roughly 1.65-3.26 eV) produces a wavelength inside the 380-750 nm visible range at all.

Connects to Chapter 17: A light-emitting diode emits photons via radiative recombination with energy close to the material's band gap, giving emission wavelength \(\lambda=hc/E_g\approx1240/E_g(\text{eV})\ \text{nm}\). Practical visible LEDs require direct-gap materials with band gaps specifically chosen to land in the visible range.

Challenge Questions

1. Setting Eg to silicon's band gap (1.12 eV), where does the computed emission wavelength fall?

Correct. 1240/1.12 ≈ 1107 nm, well into the infrared — invisible to the human eye (and silicon is a poor emitter regardless, being indirect-gap).

Compute 1240/1.12 and check whether the result falls inside the 380-750 nm visible range.

2. As Eg increases from red-emitting toward blue-emitting values, what happens to the emission wavelength?

Correct. Since λ = 1240/Eg, wavelength and band gap are inversely related — larger Eg means shorter (bluer) wavelength.
