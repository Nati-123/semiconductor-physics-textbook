Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

Try This

  • Try this: Drag the position marker slider across all three stacked charts and watch how the charge, field, and potential values at that point relate to each other.
  • Predict: Before setting NA much larger than ND, predict which side (p or n) will end up with the wider share of the depletion width.
  • Observe: The area under the ρ(x) rectangle on each side is always equal and opposite; the area under the E(x) triangle always equals the same \(V_{bi}\) value shown in ψ(x)'s final value.

Connects to Chapter 14: Applying Poisson's equation to the depletion approximation's depletion charge density \(\rho(x)\) gives the triangular junction electric field \(E(x)\). Integrating the field, together with the charge-neutrality condition \(N_Ax_p=N_Dx_n\), pins down the depletion width \(W=x_n+x_p\) and how it splits between the two sides.

What Each Control Does

NA, ND
Set the acceptor and donor doping concentrations (log scale, \(10^{15}\)-\(10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\)); the more lightly doped side always gets the wider share of \(W\).
Position marker
Reads off \(\rho(x)\), \(E(x)\), and \(\psi(x)\) at a chosen point across the depletion region.

Challenge Questions

1. What does the final (flat) value of ψ(x) on the n-side represent?

Correct. ψ(x) is the running integral of −E(x), so its total rise from the p-side to the n-side is exactly the built-in potential Vbi.

Recall that ψ(x) is built by integrating the electric field — what quantity does that total integral equal?

2. If NA is much larger than ND, where does most of the depletion width W reside?

Correct. Charge neutrality requires NA·xp = ND·xn; if NA ≫ ND, then xn must be much larger than xp to keep the exposed charge balanced.

Use the charge-neutrality relation NA·xp = ND·xn to see which width must grow when one doping is much larger.
