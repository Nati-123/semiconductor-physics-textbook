<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 1</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">What defines a direct bandgap material?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum occur at the same crystal momentum k
2. The conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum occur at different crystal momenta
3. The material has no band gap at all
4. The material conducts electricity like a metal
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: A</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">A direct bandgap material has its conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum at the same crystal momentum k, allowing a photon alone to drive a vertical band-edge transition.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Direct Bandgap</p>
</div>
</details>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 2</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">Which of these materials is an indirect bandgap material?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. GaAs
2. InP
3. Silicon
4. All of the above are direct-gap
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: C</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Silicon's conduction-band minimum sits away from k=0 (near the Brillouin zone's X point) while its valence-band maximum sits at k=0, making it indirect. GaAs and InP are both direct-gap III-V compounds.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Indirect Bandgap</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 3</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">Why must a photon-driven band-to-band transition be nearly vertical on an E-k diagram?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because a photon carries negligible crystal momentum compared to typical electron crystal momenta
2. Because photons cannot interact with electrons at all
3. Because vertical transitions have zero energy
4. Because E-k diagrams only allow vertical lines to be drawn
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: A</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">A photon's momentum, hc/λ, is roughly three orders of magnitude smaller than typical crystal momenta, so conservation of crystal momentum forces a photon-only transition to have Δk ≈ 0 — a vertical line on the E-k diagram.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> E-K Diagram</p>
</div>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 4</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">What extra particle does an indirect band-to-band transition require, beyond a photon?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A second photon
2. A phonon, to supply the missing crystal momentum
3. A neutrino
4. Nothing — indirect transitions need only a photon
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Because the band edges are offset in k, a phonon (a quantum of lattice vibration) must supply or absorb the crystal momentum difference Δk, making indirect transitions intrinsically less probable than direct ones.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Indirect Bandgap</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 5</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">How is effective mass \(m^*\) defined?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. \(m^* = \hbar^2/(d^2E/dk^2)\), from the curvature of a band at an extremum
2. \(m^* = E/c^2\)
3. \(m^*\) is always equal to the free-electron mass \(m_0\)
4. \(m^* = \hbar k\)
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: A</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Effective mass is defined from a band's curvature at an extremum, \(1/m^*=(1/\hbar^2)\,d^2E/dk^2\), letting electrons there be treated with ordinary Newtonian mechanics.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Effective Mass</p>
</div>
</details>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 6</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">A band curves very sharply near its minimum (large \(d^2E/dk^2\)). What does this imply about the effective mass there?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The effective mass is large
2. The effective mass is small
3. Curvature has no relation to effective mass
4. The effective mass is negative
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Since \(m^*=\hbar^2/(d^2E/dk^2)\), effective mass is inversely proportional to curvature — a sharply-curved band gives a small effective mass, as in GaAs's conduction band.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Effective Mass</p>
</div>
</details>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 7</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">What is the practical benefit of defining effective mass?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It lets electrons and holes near a band extremum be treated with ordinary Newtonian mechanics, \(F=m^*a\)
2. It eliminates the need for the Schrödinger equation entirely, at all energies
3. It proves that electrons have no mass
4. It only applies to free particles, not electrons in crystals
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: A</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Effective mass hides the complicated periodic-potential band structure inside one number, letting an electron near a band extremum respond to forces exactly like a classical free particle of mass \(m^*\).</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Effective Mass</p>
</div>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 8</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">What does the density of states \(g(E)\) represent?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The number of allowed electron states per unit volume per unit energy
2. The probability that a state is occupied
3. The total number of electrons in a crystal
4. The band gap energy
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: A</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">\(g(E)\,dE\) is the number of allowed electron states per unit volume with energy between E and E+dE — it says nothing about occupation, only availability.</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 9</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">How does the conduction-band density of states \(g_c(E)\) depend on energy above the band edge, for a parabolic band?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is constant, independent of energy
2. It grows linearly with energy
3. It grows as the square root of energy above the band edge
4. It decreases as energy increases
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: C</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">\(g_c(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}(2m_e^*/\hbar^2)^{3/2}\sqrt{E-E_c}\) — a direct result of counting states in three-dimensional k-space, growing as the square root of energy above \(E_c\).</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Density of States</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 10</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">What is the Fermi-Dirac distribution \(f(E)\)?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The number of states at energy E
2. The probability that a state of energy E is occupied by an electron
3. The band gap energy
4. The effective mass of an electron at energy E
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">\(f(E)=1/[1+\exp((E-E_F)/k_BT)]\) gives the probability that a state of energy E is occupied, parameterized by the Fermi level \(E_F\).</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Fermi Level</p>
</div>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 11</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">What is \(f(E)\) exactly at \(E=E_F\), for any temperature \(T>0\)?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 0
2. 1
3. 1/2
4. Undefined
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: C</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Setting \(E=E_F\) makes the exponential term equal 1, so \(f(E_F)=1/(1+1)=1/2\) — the Fermi level is, by definition, the energy at which occupation probability is exactly one-half.</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">In an intrinsic semiconductor, the Fermi level sits inside the band gap, where \(g(E)=0\). Is this a contradiction?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Insulators have a partially-filled band; semiconductors do not

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 17</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">What defines a semimetal band structure?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A very large band gap, over 4 eV

3. A completely empty valence band

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">A semimetal's bands touch or slightly overlap, but with very low density of states right at the overlap energy, giving a much lower carrier density than a true metal — bismuth and graphite are examples.</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 18</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">Magnesium has two valence electrons per atom, which naive electron counting suggests should exactly fill one band (making it an insulator), yet magnesium conducts well. Why?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Magnesium has no band gap theory applicable to it
2. Its valence and conduction bands overlap in energy, leaving both partially filled and the Fermi level inside a band
3. Magnesium is actually an insulator in reality
4. Magnesium has an unusually large band gap
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>

<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Because magnesium's bands overlap in energy rather than being separated by a gap, electrons partially populate both bands, leaving the Fermi level inside a partially-filled band — the metal band structure condition, despite the simple electron-counting argument.</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 19</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">Why are GaAs-based devices much more efficient LEDs and laser diodes than silicon-based devices?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. GaAs has a larger band gap than silicon
2. GaAs is direct-gap, so a photon alone can drive band-edge emission, while silicon's indirect gap requires a much less probable phonon-assisted process
3. Silicon has no conduction band
4. GaAs has a smaller effective mass, which is unrelated to light emission
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">GaAs's direct gap allows efficient photon-only recombination at the band edge, while silicon's indirect gap requires a much rarer phonon-assisted process, making silicon a poor light emitter despite being an excellent solar-cell absorber.</p>
