Try This

  • Try this: Raise the reverse-bias slider from 0 V and watch Cj fall while the depletion-region schematic visibly widens.
  • Predict: Before doubling the junction area A, predict whether Cj will double, stay the same, or halve.
  • Observe: The Cj vs. VR curve falls steeply at first, then flattens — Cj is proportional to \(1/\sqrt{V_{bi}+V_R}\), a square-root dependence, not linear.

Connects to Chapter 14: The depletion region behaves electrically like a parallel-plate capacitor with plate separation \(W\), giving the junction capacitance \(C_j=\varepsilon A/W\). Because reverse bias widens \(W\), \(C_j\) falls under reverse bias — the physical basis of the varactor diode used as a voltage-tunable capacitor in RF circuits, and a direct preview of Chapter 15.

What Each Control Does

NA, ND
Set doping concentrations, which set \(V_{bi}\) and the doping term in \(W\).
A
Sets the junction cross-sectional area, which scales \(C_j\) directly.
VR
Sets the reverse bias voltage, which widens \(W\) and lowers \(C_j\).

Challenge Questions

1. If the junction area A is doubled with everything else fixed, what happens to Cj?

Correct. Cj = εA/W is directly proportional to area A, since W does not depend on A.

Look at where A appears in the Cj formula — is it inside a square root or a direct multiplier?

2. Why does reverse bias decrease junction capacitance?

Correct. Since Cj = εA/W, widening W (as reverse bias does) directly lowers Cj, just as pulling apart a parallel-plate capacitor's plates lowers its capacitance.

Think of the depletion region as a parallel-plate capacitor — what physical distance does reverse bias change?
