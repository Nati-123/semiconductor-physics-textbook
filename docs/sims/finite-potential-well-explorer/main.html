Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

Try This

  • Try this: Set well depth V₀ to its minimum — notice only 1 bound state exists, and its wavefunction extends visibly outside the well walls.
  • Predict: Before increasing V₀ toward its maximum, predict whether the number of bound states will increase or decrease.
  • Observe: Increase V₀ until 3-4 bound states appear, then select each one in turn and watch the wavefunction gain one more node each time (n=1: no nodes, n=2: 1 node, n=3: 2 nodes, ...).
  • Compare: Check "Compare to infinite well" and notice the dashed infinite-well curve is forced to exactly zero at the walls, while the solid finite-well curve visibly decays into the walls instead.
  • Explain: Explain why the ground state energy of the finite well is always somewhat lower than the corresponding infinite-well energy for the same L.

Connects to Chapter 2: This MicroSim solves the finite-square-well matching equations exactly: even-parity bound states satisfy \(z\tan z=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2}\) and odd-parity states satisfy \(z\cot z=-\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2}\), where \(z=kL/2\) and \(z_0=(L/2\hbar)\sqrt{2mV_0}\). Outside the well, the wavefunction decays as \(e^{-\kappa(|x|-L/2)}\) rather than dropping to exactly zero — this is exactly the "Finite Potential Wells" text discusses: real confining potentials (a doped-atom electron, a thin semiconductor layer) always let the wavefunction leak into the classically forbidden region.

What Each Control Does

Bound state dropdown
Selects which bound state's wavefunction to plot; the list updates automatically as L and V₀ change the number of available states.
Compare to infinite well checkbox
Overlays the corresponding infinite-well eigenfunction (dashed) for direct comparison against the finite-well curve (solid).
Well width L slider
Sets the width of the square well from 0.3 nm to 1.5 nm.
Well depth V₀ slider
Sets the potential energy of the walls from 0.2 eV to 3.0 eV — deeper or wider wells support more bound states.

Challenge Questions

1. What happens to the wavefunction of a finite-well bound state at the well's walls?

Because V₀ is finite, the wavefunction and its derivative must be continuous at the walls, giving an exponential decay \(e^{-\kappa(|x|-L/2)}\) outside rather than an abrupt drop to zero.

Look at where the solid blue curve crosses the well walls — does it stop abruptly or continue smoothly?

2. Increasing the well depth V₀ (holding L fixed) generally does what to the number of bound states?

A deeper well raises \(z_0\), which extends the range over which the matching-equation branches can find a root, so more bound states fit.

Drag V₀ from its minimum to its maximum and watch the "Bound states found" readout update.

3. Comparing a finite well to the infinite well of the same width L, which has the lower ground-state energy?

Because the finite-well wavefunction is allowed to spread slightly beyond the walls, it is less sharply confined than the infinite well's, which lowers its kinetic energy and therefore its total energy.

Check "Compare to infinite well" and note how much wider the finite-well curve's effective extent is.

4. How does the number of internal nodes change from the n=1 bound state to the n=2 bound state?

Just like the infinite well and other bound-state systems, node count increases by one with each successive energy level — select each available state in the dropdown and count the internal zero-crossings.

Select n=1 and n=2 from the dropdown in turn and count how many times the curve crosses zero inside the well.

5. What determines whether a bound state is labeled "even" or "odd" parity?

Even-parity states use \(\cos(kx)\) inside the well (mirror-symmetric about x=0); odd-parity states use \(\sin(kx)\) (antisymmetric) — this happens to alternate with n in this labeling, but the defining property is the symmetry, not the number itself.

Look at the parity label shown next to each energy level and compare the curve shapes for an even vs. an odd state.
