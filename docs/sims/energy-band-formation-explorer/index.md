<h2 style="color: #5A3EED !important; border-bottom: 2px solid #5A3EED; padding-bottom: 0.3rem; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 2rem;">Description</h2>

<p style="color: #555; line-height: 1.85; font-size: 1.02rem; margin-bottom: 1.2rem;">
This MicroSim visualizes the physical origin of band formation, before the Kronig-Penney model formalizes it mathematically. Every isolated atom has the same sharp, discrete energy levels. As N identical atoms are brought together, their wavefunctions begin to overlap, and each level splits into N closely-spaced sub-levels — modeled here as a simple finite tight-binding spectrum, <span class="arithmatex">\(E_j = E_0 \pm t(d)\cos\!\big(\tfrac{\pi(j+1)}{N+1}\big)\)</span>, where the coupling strength <span class="arithmatex">\(t(d)\)</span> grows as the interatomic spacing <span class="arithmatex">\(d\)</span> shrinks. A "fan diagram" traces all N split states continuously as spacing varies, so you can watch a single sharp line (isolated atoms) spread into a band of N discrete states, and — for large N — into what looks like a continuous band exactly like the ones the Kronig-Penney model produces.
</p>
