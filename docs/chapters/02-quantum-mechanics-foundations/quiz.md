<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 2</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">According to the de Broglie relation <span class="arithmatex">\(\lambda = h/p\)</span>, what happens to a particle's wavelength if its momentum is doubled?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Wavelength doubles
2. Wavelength is halved
3. Wavelength stays the same
4. Wavelength quadruples
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>

<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Since <span class="arithmatex">\(\lambda = h/p\)</span>, wavelength is inversely proportional to momentum. Doubling <span class="arithmatex">\(p\)</span> halves <span class="arithmatex">\(\lambda\)</span>.</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 3</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">Why is the wave nature of a thrown baseball never observed in everyday experience, while the wave nature of an electron is readily observed in diffraction experiments?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Baseballs do not have momentum
2. The baseball's much larger mass gives it a de Broglie wavelength far too small to ever detect
3. Baseballs are electrically neutral
4. Diffraction only occurs for charged particles
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Since <span class="arithmatex">\(\lambda = h/(mv)\)</span>, a baseball's large mass drives its de Broglie wavelength down to roughly <span class="arithmatex">\(10^{-34}\)</span> m, about twenty orders of magnitude smaller than a proton — utterly unobservable by any conceivable experiment.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> de Broglie Wavelength</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">According to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, what happens to the minimum uncertainty in momentum as the position uncertainty <span class="arithmatex">\(\Delta x\)</span> decreases?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It also decreases
2. It stays constant
3. It increases
4. It becomes exactly zero
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Since <span class="arithmatex">\(\Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \hbar/2\)</span>, decreasing <span class="arithmatex">\(\Delta x\)</span> forces the minimum allowed <span class="arithmatex">\(\Delta p\)</span> to increase — the two are inversely related.</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is quantum mechanics' equation of motion, determining the allowed wavefunctions and energies for a given potential
2. It defines the electric field produced by a point charge
3. It calculates the classical trajectory of a particle

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>

<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: A</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">The Schrödinger equation, <span class="arithmatex">\(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''+V\psi=E\psi\)</span>, plays the role for matter waves that Newton's laws play in classical mechanics: solving it for a given potential <span class="arithmatex">\(V(x)\)</span> determines the allowed wavefunctions and energies simultaneously.</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 8</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">Why can the wavefunction <span class="arithmatex">\(\psi(x)\)</span> itself not be directly interpreted as a probability?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is always equal to zero
2. It is in general complex-valued and can be negative, so it cannot represent a probability directly
3. It only exists for macroscopic objects
4. It has no mathematical definition
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>

<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">A probability must be a real, non-negative number, but <span class="arithmatex">\(\psi(x)\)</span> is in general complex-valued and can take negative values. <p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Since <span class="arithmatex">\(E_n = n^2h^2/(8mL^2)\)</span>, energy is proportional to <span class="arithmatex">\(1/L^2\)</span>; shrinking the box raises every energy level, consistent with the uncertainty-principle argument that confinement raises minimum momentum and kinetic energy.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Particle in a Box</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 16</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">Why must the ground state of a particle in a box (<span class="arithmatex">\(n=1\)</span>) have a strictly positive energy rather than zero?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because the box has a finite wall height
2. Because <span class="arithmatex">\(n=0\)</span> would give the trivial, unnormalizable zero wavefunction, and confinement forces a nonzero minimum kinetic energy
3. Because energy is positive by definition in all physical systems
4. Because <span class="arithmatex">\(n=0\)</span> is not an integer
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Setting <span class="arithmatex">\(n=0\)</span> gives <span class="arithmatex">\(\psi=0\)</span> everywhere, which cannot be normalized and describes no particle at all. The smallest physically valid state is <span class="arithmatex">\(n=1\)</span>, whose strictly positive zero-point energy is consistent with the uncertainty principle: confinement always forces some minimum momentum and kinetic energy.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Particle in a Box</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 17</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">What does quantum tunneling allow a particle to do that is forbidden classically?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Travel faster than the speed of light
2. Have a nonzero probability of appearing on the far side of a potential barrier even when its energy is less than the barrier height
3. Have permanently negative kinetic energy
4. Violate conservation of energy
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Quantum tunneling gives a particle a nonzero probability of being transmitted through a barrier taller than its own energy — a purely quantum effect with no classical analog, since a classical particle would simply reflect.</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 18</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">According to the approximate tunneling formula <span class="arithmatex">\(T\approx e^{-2\kappa L}\)</span>, what happens to the transmission probability if the barrier width <span class="arithmatex">\(L\)</span> is doubled, with <span class="arithmatex">\(\kappa\)</span> unchanged?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. T doubles
2. T is roughly squared, becoming much smaller
3. T stays exactly the same
4. T becomes exactly 1
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Since <span class="arithmatex">\(T\approx e^{-2\kappa L}\)</span>, doubling <span class="arithmatex">\(L\)</span> doubles the exponent, so the new transmission probability is the square of the old one: <span class="arithmatex">\(T_{\text{new}}=(T_{\text{old}})^2\)</span>, a dramatic decrease for any small <span class="arithmatex">\(T_{\text{old}}\)</span>.</p>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 19</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">How does a finite potential well differ from the idealized infinite square well (particle in a box)?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A finite well has no allowed energy levels at all
2. In a finite well, the wavefunction decays smoothly into the classically forbidden region outside the well rather than dropping exactly to zero at the walls
3. A finite well only applies to photons, not massive particles
4. A finite well has infinitely many walls
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Unlike the infinite well, where <span class="arithmatex">\(\psi\)</span> is forced to exactly zero at the walls, a finite well allows the wavefunction to decay smoothly into the classically forbidden region outside, giving a small but nonzero probability of finding the particle there — the same physics underlying quantum tunneling.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Potential Well</p>
</div>
</details>

<p style="color: #1565C0; font-weight: 700; font-size: 1.08rem; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 14px;">Question 20</p>

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">In the particle-in-a-box derivation, what specific mathematical consequence follows from imposing the boundary condition <span class="arithmatex">\(\psi(L)=0\)</span> on the general solution <span class="arithmatex">\(\psi(x)=A\sin(kx)\)</span>?</p>

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It has no effect on the solution
2. It forces <span class="arithmatex">\(\sin(kL)=0\)</span>, which only holds when <span class="arithmatex">\(kL=n\pi\)</span>, quantizing the allowed values of <span class="arithmatex">\(k\)</span> and hence the energy
3. It forces the constant <span class="arithmatex">\(A\)</span> to equal exactly 1
4. It requires the potential energy to be negative
</div>

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Answer</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #2E7D32; font-weight: 700; margin-top: 0;">Correct Answer: B</p>
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 0;">Requiring <span class="arithmatex">\(\psi(L)=A\sin(kL)=0\)</span> (with <span class="arithmatex">\(A\neq0\)</span>) forces <span class="arithmatex">\(\sin(kL)=0\)</span>, true only when <span class="arithmatex">\(kL\)</span> is an integer multiple of <span class="arithmatex">\(\pi\)</span>. This is the boundary condition directly responsible for quantizing both <span class="arithmatex">\(k\)</span> and, through <span class="arithmatex">\(E=\hbar^2k^2/2m\)</span>, the allowed energies.</p>
<p style="color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 0; margin-top: 8px;"><strong>Concept Tested:</strong> Boundary Conditions</p>
