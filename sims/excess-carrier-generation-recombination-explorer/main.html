Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

What to Observe

  • While generation is ON, Δn rises smoothly toward a steady-state value Δn_ss = G·τ and then flattens out — it never grows without bound.
  • Δp(t) (dashed, red) sits exactly on top of Δn(t) (solid, purple) at every instant — under low-level injection, charge neutrality forces the excess electron and hole populations to stay equal.
  • After you click "Turn Generation OFF," a green marker appears exactly one lifetime τ after the "generation off" line, showing Δn has fallen to about 37% (1/e) of its value at that moment.
  • The "Generation source" dropdown jumps the generation rate G to a realistic optical vs. thermal preset — notice how much larger Δn_ss is under optical generation.

Predict First

  • Predict: Before clicking "Turn Generation OFF," predict roughly how many lifetimes it will take for Δn to fall below 10% of its value at that moment. (Hint: each lifetime removes a fixed fraction, not a fixed amount.)
  • Predict: Before switching the source preset from Optical to Thermal, predict whether Δn_ss will go up or down, and by roughly how many orders of magnitude.

Guided Exploration

  • Try this: Leave generation ON and drag the time marker across the window — confirm Δn approaches, but never exceeds, Δn_ss.
  • Try this: Click "Turn Generation OFF," then drag the "Turns off at" slider — watch the whole decay phase shift with it, always starting from wherever Δn had reached when generation stopped.
  • Observe: A longer minority carrier lifetime τ makes both the rise and the decay noticeably more gradual, since τ sets the natural timescale of both phases.
  • Observe: The Δn/N readout at the bottom previews the injection-level ratio explored fully in the next MicroSim (Injection Level Classifier) — keep N fixed and increase G to see this ratio grow.

Connects to Chapter 13: Excess carriers are carriers above the equilibrium concentration, created by carrier generation (optical or thermal) and removed by carrier recombination. While generation is active, excess carriers build up toward a steady state where generation and recombination balance, \(\Delta n_{ss}=G\tau\); once generation stops, they decay exponentially with time constant equal to the minority carrier lifetime τ, and the instantaneous recombination rate is simply \(R=\Delta n/\tau\).

What Each Control Does

Generation source
Jumps the generation rate G to a representative optical (light pulse) or thermal (background) preset — you can still fine-tune G afterward.
G (generation rate)
Sets the generation rate, in carriers created per cm³ per second; the steady-state excess concentration is derived from it as Δn_ss = G·τ.
τ (lifetime)
Sets the minority carrier lifetime controlling both the rise and decay timescales.
N (background doping, reference)
Sets a reference majority carrier concentration, used only to compute the Δn/N injection-level ratio shown in the readout — it does not affect the Δn(t) curve itself.
Turn Generation ON/OFF button
Toggles whether generation is currently active. ON shows pure approach to steady state; OFF adds a decay phase starting at the "Turns off at" time.
Turns off at
Only active when generation is OFF — sets, in multiples of τ, how long generation ran before it stopped.
Time marker
Scrubs through the rise and/or decay phase, reading off Δn, Δp, and the instantaneous recombination rate.

Challenge Questions

1. After generation is turned off, what does the excess carrier concentration do?

Once generation stops, recombination alone governs the excess carriers, producing an exponential decay Δn(t)=Δn(Ton)·exp(-(t-Ton)/τ).

Click "Turn Generation OFF" and watch the curve after the dashed "generation off" line — it falls off smoothly, not instantly or linearly.

2. You double the generation rate G while keeping τ fixed. What happens to the steady-state excess concentration Δn_ss?

Δn_ss = G·τ is a direct proportionality: doubling G with τ fixed exactly doubles the steady-state excess concentration.

Watch the "Δn_ss = G·τ" readout at the top as you move the G slider with τ fixed.

3. At the point marked one lifetime after generation turns off, roughly what fraction of the excess concentration (at the moment generation stopped) remains?

By definition of the exponential time constant, exactly one lifetime after generation stops, Δn has fallen to 1/e ≈ 37% of its starting value for the decay.

Look at the green-marked point on the decay curve and compare its label to the value right where generation turned off.

Key Takeaway: Excess carrier concentration is a balance between two competing rates: generation builds it up toward a steady state Δn_ss = G·τ, and recombination tears it back down with time constant τ once generation stops. The same lifetime τ sets the timescale of both processes, and because low-level injection preserves charge neutrality, Δn and Δp rise and fall together throughout.
