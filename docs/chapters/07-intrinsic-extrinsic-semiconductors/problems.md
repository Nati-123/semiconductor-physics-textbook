### Problem 2

What is the key structural difference between an elemental and a compound semiconductor?

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">

## Medium

### Problem 9

Explain why a broken covalent bond in an intrinsic semiconductor always produces exactly one electron and one hole, never one without the other.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">A broken bond and its resulting vacancy are two descriptions of the same single event: the freed electron becomes the free carrier, and the vacancy it leaves behind is the hole. There is no separate mechanism that could create one without the other in a pure crystal.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 10

Indium arsenide (InAs) is a compound semiconductor. Using periodic table group numbers, explain why In and As can pair to form a stable semiconductor.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Indium is Group III (3 valence electrons) and arsenic is Group V (5 valence electrons); paired together they average 4 valence electrons per atom, matching silicon's covalent-bonding count and allowing the same zincblende bonding geometry as GaAs.</p>

### Problem 11

Using silicon's Varshni constants (\(E_g(0)=1.166\) eV, \(\alpha=4.73\times10^{-4}\) eV/K, \(\beta=636\) K), find silicon's band gap at \(T=100\) K.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;"><span class="arithmatex">\(E_g(100) = 1.166 - \dfrac{(4.73\times10^{-4})(100)^2}{100+636} = 1.166 - \dfrac{4.73}{736} = 1.166-0.0064 \approx 1.160\ \text{eV}\)</span>. At low temperature the gap is very close to its \(T=0\) extrapolated value.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 12

A silicon sample is doped with boron at \(N_A=5\times10^{15}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). Using \(N_{Si}\approx5\times10^{22}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), find the ratio of silicon atoms to acceptor atoms.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>

### Problem 13

Explain why GaAs requires more careful stoichiometric control during crystal growth than silicon does.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Silicon has only one atomic species, so there is no ratio to control. GaAs must be grown with gallium and arsenic in very nearly a 1:1 ratio; deviations introduce antisite defects (a Ga atom on an As site, or vice versa) that disrupt bonding and degrade performance.</p>
</div>

### Problem 14

Using \(m^*/m_0=0.12\) and \(\varepsilon_r=16.2\) (germanium's approximate values), estimate the hydrogenic donor ionization energy for germanium.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;"><span class="arithmatex">\(E_D \approx 13.6\times\dfrac{0.12}{16.2^2} = 13.6\times\dfrac{0.12}{262.4} \approx 13.6\times4.57\times10^{-4} \approx 0.0062\ \text{eV} = 6.2\ \text{meV}\)</span>, the right order of magnitude compared to germanium's real measured donor ionization energies (roughly 10-13 meV).</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 15

Why does silicon dominate digital integrated circuits despite germanium having higher carrier mobility?

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Silicon's much higher melting point gives it a wider thermal processing window for high-temperature fabrication steps, and its excellent native oxide (SiO2) is essential for building high-quality MOSFETs — advantages that outweigh germanium's mobility edge for mainstream digital manufacturing.</p>

### Problem 16

At room temperature, is a typical donor or acceptor atom in silicon mostly ionized or mostly un-ionized? Justify your answer using the ionization energies discussed in this chapter.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Mostly ionized. Measured donor/acceptor ionization energies in silicon (tens of meV) are comparable to or only somewhat larger than room-temperature thermal energy (<span class="arithmatex">\(k_BT\approx26\)</span> meV), so most dopant atoms have enough thermal energy to ionize — the basis of the "complete ionization" assumption used starting in Chapter 8.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 17

A hypothetical semiconductor has intrinsic carrier concentration \(n_i=2\times10^{9}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) at room temperature and is doped with a donor concentration \(N_D=3\times10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). By what factor does doping increase the free carrier concentration compared to the intrinsic case (assuming complete ionization and that donor electrons dominate)?

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;"><span class="arithmatex">\(N_D/n_i = (3\times10^{16})/(2\times10^{9}) = 1.5\times10^{7}\)</span> — doping increases the carrier concentration by about 15 million times, illustrating why extrinsic behavior overwhelms intrinsic behavior at any practical doping level.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 18

Explain, using the hydrogenic model, why a material with a very large dielectric constant but only a modestly reduced effective mass would still be expected to have a very small dopant ionization energy.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Because <span class="arithmatex">\(E_D\propto(m^*/m_0)/\varepsilon_r^2\)</span>, the dielectric constant appears squared in the denominator, so it dominates the result far more strongly than a modest change in effective mass would. A large <span class="arithmatex">\(\varepsilon_r\)</span> screens the Coulomb attraction so effectively that ionization energy drops sharply, even if the effective mass reduction alone is modest.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 19

A crystal grower produces a GaAs sample that is slightly As-rich rather than perfectly stoichiometric. Predict, qualitatively, what kind of defect this introduces and why this is a concern unique to compound (rather than elemental) semiconductors.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">An As-rich sample has more arsenic atoms than needed for perfect 1:1 stoichiometry, so some excess As atoms must occupy sites that should hold Ga — an antisite defect (As on a Ga site). Elemental semiconductors like silicon have only one atomic species, so there is no stoichiometric ratio to violate in the first place; this is a concern unique to compound semiconductors.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 20

A silicon sample must be doped so that dopant atoms are no more than 1 in 10 million silicon atoms. Using \(N_{Si}\approx5\times10^{22}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), find the maximum allowed doping concentration, and identify which of this chapter's earlier worked examples used a doping level in this same range.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">

<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Maximum <span class="arithmatex">\(N_D = N_{Si}/10^7 = (5\times10^{22})/10^7 = 5\times10^{15}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\)</span>. This matches the boron doping level used in the earlier acceptor-ratio worked example, which also produced a ratio of about 10 million silicon atoms per dopant atom.</p>
