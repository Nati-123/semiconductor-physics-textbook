Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

Try This

  • Try this: In "Bond Types" view, switch between Covalent, Ionic, and Metallic. In Covalent, notice the central Si atom shares an electron pair with each of its 4 neighbors, and the faint stub lines hint that each neighbor keeps bonding outward the same way, throughout the crystal.
  • Predict: Before switching to Ionic, predict which atom (Na or Cl) will end up positively charged and which negatively charged, based on their valence electron counts.
  • Observe: In Ionic, notice that every ion attracts all of its neighbors, not just one partner — this non-directional attraction is why ionic solids form extended lattices instead of isolated pairs.
  • Compare: Switch to "Crystal Order" view and compare Single Crystal, Polycrystalline, and Amorphous side by side — notice that Polycrystalline still has straight grid lines within each grain, while Amorphous has none anywhere.
  • Observe: In Single Crystal mode, toggle "Show defects" and identify the vacancy (dashed outline, no atom), interstitial (small orange atom between grid sites), and substitutional (green atom on a regular site) defects using the legend.
  • Explain: Explain why the grain boundaries in Polycrystalline mode are exactly where two differently-rotated grids meet.

Connects to Chapter 4: The Bond Types view illustrates the three bonding mechanisms this chapter derives from valence electron count: covalent (a central Si atom sharing an electron pair with each of 4 neighbors — 4 bonds x 2 electrons = the octet — with the pattern continuing throughout the crystal), ionic (complete electron transfer repeated across an extended Na⁺/Cl⁻ lattice, not just one pair), and metallic (a grid of fixed positive ion cores surrounded by a delocalized electron sea). The Crystal Order view illustrates how real crystals deviate from the perfect periodicity assumed in Chapter 3: a polycrystalline solid is a mosaic of internally-periodic grains at random orientations, an amorphous solid has no long-range order at all, and even a defect-free-looking single crystal can contain vacancy, interstitial, and substitutional point defects — the last of which becomes the deliberate tool of doping in Chapter 8.

What Each Control Does

View dropdown
Switches the entire MicroSim between "Bond Types" and "Crystal Order" mode, showing the relevant second control for each.
Bond type dropdown (Bond Types view)
Selects which of the three bonding mechanisms — Covalent, Ionic, or Metallic — is illustrated.
Order dropdown (Crystal Order view)
Selects Single Crystal, Polycrystalline, or Amorphous atomic arrangement.
Show defects checkbox (Crystal Order view)
When Single Crystal is selected, overlays a vacancy, an interstitial, and a substitutional point defect onto the otherwise perfect grid, with a legend.

Challenge Questions

1. In the Ionic bond view, why does chlorine end up negatively charged?

Sodium's single, loosely-held valence electron transfers completely to chlorine, which needs just one more electron to complete its octet — chlorine gains a negative charge (Cl⁻) and sodium is left positive (Na⁺).

Select "Ionic" and follow the electron-transfer arrow from Na to Cl.

2. What visually distinguishes the Metallic bond view from the Covalent bond view?

The metallic view's orange electron dots are spread across the whole ion-core grid (the "electron sea"), while the covalent view's two electrons are localized in a cloud between exactly two atoms.

Switch between Covalent and Metallic and compare where the orange electron dots are drawn.

3. In Crystal Order view, what does a grain boundary in Polycrystalline mode represent?

Each of the four quadrants in Polycrystalline mode is drawn with its own rotated grid; the bold lines mark the grain boundaries where these differently-oriented periodic regions meet.

Select Polycrystalline and look closely at the grid line angles on either side of the bold boundary lines.

4. In Single Crystal mode with "Show defects" checked, which marker represents a vacancy?

A vacancy is a missing atom, drawn as a dashed, empty outline at a site that should otherwise hold a normal (purple) atom — check the legend in the top-right of the canvas.

Turn on "Show defects" and read the legend box in the corner of the canvas, which labels each of the three defect markers.
