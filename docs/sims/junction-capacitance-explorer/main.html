What to Observe

  • The right-hand schematic is literally a parallel-plate capacitor: its plate separation is drawn proportional to the actual depletion width W, so you can see \(C_j=\varepsilon A/W\) growing and shrinking exactly as the plates move apart and together.
  • Raise the reverse-bias slider from 0 V and watch C_j fall while the depletion-region schematic visibly widens — the direct visual link between "more reverse bias" and "less capacitance."
  • The C_j vs. V_R curve falls steeply at first, then flattens — C_j is proportional to \(1/\sqrt{V_{bi}+V_R}\), a square-root dependence, not linear or exponential.

Guided Exploration

Connects to Chapter 14: The depletion region behaves electrically like a parallel-plate capacitor with plate separation \(W\), giving the junction capacitance \(C_j=\varepsilon A/W\). Because reverse bias widens \(W\), \(C_j\) falls under reverse bias — the physical basis of the varactor diode used as a voltage-tunable capacitor in RF circuits, and a direct preview of Chapter 15.

What Each Control Does

N_A, N_D
Set doping concentrations, which set \(V_{bi}\) and the doping term in \(W\).
Area A
Sets the junction cross-sectional area, which scales \(C_j\) directly.
Reverse bias V_R
Sets the reverse bias voltage, which widens \(W\) and lowers \(C_j\).

Challenge Questions

1. If the junction area A is doubled with everything else fixed, what happens to Cj?

Correct. Cj = εA/W is directly proportional to area A, since W does not depend on A.

Look at where A appears in the Cj formula — is it inside a square root or a direct multiplier?

2. Why does reverse bias decrease junction capacitance?

Correct. Since Cj = εA/W, widening W (as reverse bias does) directly lowers Cj, just as pulling apart a parallel-plate capacitor's plates lowers its capacitance.

Think of the depletion region as a parallel-plate capacitor — what physical distance does reverse bias change?

3. Why does the C_j vs. V_R curve flatten out at high reverse bias instead of continuing to fall at the same rate?

Because W grows as √(V_bi+V_R), each additional volt of reverse bias adds proportionally less to W (and removes proportionally less from C_j) once V_R is already large — the classic flattening shape of a square-root/inverse-square-root curve.

Look at the exponent inside the W formula — is V_R inside a square root, or multiplied directly?

Key Takeaway: A reverse-biased p-n junction is a real, voltage-controlled capacitor — not just an analogy. Increasing reverse bias widens the depletion region exactly like pulling apart parallel plates, which is why \(C_j=\varepsilon A/W\) falls (following a square-root law, not a linear one) as \(V_R\) increases. This voltage-tunable capacitance is deliberately exploited in varactor diodes.
