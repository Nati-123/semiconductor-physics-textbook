What to Observe

  • Three preset buttons — Symmetric, p+–n, and p–n+ — jump straight to the doping ratios that most clearly demonstrate the "more lightly doped side gets the wider depletion width" rule, without hand-tuning both sliders.
  • All three charts share the same x-axis and the same dashed boundary lines at \(x=-x_p\) (p-side edge), \(x=0\) (metallurgical junction), and \(x=x_n\) (n-side edge) — line up any feature vertically to compare the three quantities at the same position.
  • The position marker (red dot) now spans the entire plotted range, not just the depletion region — drag it outside \(-x_p\) or \(x_n\) and confirm \(\rho=0\), \(E=0\), and \(\psi\) is flat there, exactly as the depletion approximation assumes.
  • \(E_{max}\) and \(V_{bi}\) are marked directly on the \(E(x)\) and \(\psi(x)\) charts with dashed reference lines, and the \(W\) bracket at the bottom spans the full depletion width.

Guided Exploration

  • Try this: Drag the position marker slider across all three stacked charts and watch how the charge, field, and potential values at that point relate to each other.
  • Predict: Before setting N_A much larger than N_D, predict which side (p or n) will end up with the wider share of the depletion width.
  • Observe: The area under the ρ(x) rectangle on each side is always equal and opposite; the area under the E(x) triangle always equals the same \(V_{bi}\) value marked on ψ(x)'s flat asymptote.

Connects to Chapter 14: Applying Poisson's equation to the depletion approximation's depletion charge density \(\rho(x)\) gives the triangular junction electric field \(E(x)\). Integrating the field, together with the charge-neutrality condition \(N_Ax_p=N_Dx_n\), pins down the depletion width \(W=x_n+x_p\) and how it splits between the two sides.

What Each Control Does

N_A, N_D
Set the acceptor and donor doping concentrations (log scale, \(10^{15}\)-\(10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\)); the more lightly doped side always gets the wider share of \(W\).
Position marker x
Reads off \(\rho(x)\), \(E(x)\), and \(\psi(x)\) at a chosen point across the full plotted range, inside or outside the depletion region.

Challenge Questions

1. What does the final (flat) value of ψ(x) on the n-side represent?

Correct. ψ(x) is the running integral of −E(x), so its total rise from the p-side to the n-side is exactly the built-in potential V_bi — marked directly on the chart with a dashed line.

Recall that ψ(x) is built by integrating the electric field — what quantity does that total integral equal? Check the dashed reference line's label.

2. If N_A is much larger than N_D, where does most of the depletion width W reside?

Correct. Charge neutrality requires N_A·x_p = N_D·x_n; if N_A ≫ N_D, then x_n must be much larger than x_p to keep the exposed charge balanced.

Use the charge-neutrality relation N_A·x_p = N_D·x_n to see which width must grow when one doping is much larger.

3. Drag the position marker to a point well outside the depletion region (past x_n or before −x_p). What do you see?

Outside [−x_p, x_n], the depletion approximation assumes the semiconductor is fully neutral: no net charge, no field, and a constant potential — exactly what the marker reads there.

Slide the position marker slider all the way to one end and read the info card's values.

Key Takeaway: These three curves are not independent — they are three views of the same physics, linked by calculus. ρ(x) is the source; integrating it once (Poisson's equation) gives E(x); integrating again gives ψ(x). The total area under the E(x) triangle is always exactly V_bi, and the depletion region always splits so that the more lightly doped side carries the wider share of W — both are direct, visible consequences of charge neutrality.
