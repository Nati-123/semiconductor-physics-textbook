### Problem 9

Show explicitly why a silicon atom forming 4 covalent bonds satisfies the octet rule, even though silicon itself supplies only 4 electrons.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Each of silicon's 4 bonds is a shared pair containing 2 electrons: 1 from silicon and 1 from the neighboring atom. Silicon counts both electrons of every bond it participates in toward its own octet, so <span class="arithmatex">\(4\text{ bonds}\times2\text{ electrons/bond}=8\)</span> electrons in its local environment, even though silicon's own contribution was only 4.</p>
</div>

### Problem 11

Explain why sodium tends to bond metallically with other sodium atoms, but ionically when paired with chlorine.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Among identical sodium atoms, there is no electronegativity difference to drive complete electron transfer, so each atom's loosely-bound single valence electron simply delocalizes into a shared electron sea, favoring a metallic bond. ### Problem 16

Explain qualitatively why two silicon atoms bond covalently with each other rather than ionically, even though both atoms are identical.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Ionic bonding requires a large electronegativity difference so that one atom donates and the other accepts electrons completely. </div>

### Problem 17

Derive the tetrahedral bond angle from the geometric requirement that 4 identical directions from a common center be spaced as far apart as possible, showing that this angle is \(\arccos(-1/3)\).

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Placing 4 identical unit vectors symmetrically so their sum is zero (each direction pulls equally, so the net electron-electron repulsion is balanced in every direction) is the geometric condition satisfied uniquely by the 4 vertex directions of a regular tetrahedron centered on the origin. Computing the dot product between any two such unit vectors gives <span class="arithmatex">\(\cos\theta = -1/3\)</span>, so <span class="arithmatex">\(\theta = \arccos(-1/3) \approx 109.47°\)</span>, matching the tetrahedral bond angle used throughout this chapter.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 18

A hypothetical ionic crystal has ions of charge \(+2e\) and \(-2e\) separated by \(r=0.24\) nm. Estimate its Coulomb bond energy in electron-volts, and compare it to the \(\text{Na}^+\text{Cl}^-\) result of about \(-5.1\) eV found in the chapter (charges of \(\pm e\) at \(r=0.28\) nm).

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">With charges <span class="arithmatex">\(\pm2e\)</span>: <span class="arithmatex">\(U(r) = -\dfrac{(8.99\times10^9)(2\times1.602\times10^{-19})^2}{0.24\times10^{-9}} = -3.85\times10^{-18}\ \text{J} \approx -24.0\ \text{eV}\)</span>. ### Problem 19

A larger fragment of a silicon crystal contains 20 interior silicon atoms, each fully bonded to 4 neighbors within the fragment (assume no bonds cross the fragment boundary). Find the total number of covalent bonds and the total number of shared electrons in the fragment.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Each of the 20 atoms contributes 4 bond-ends, giving <span class="arithmatex">\(20\times4=80\)</span> bond-ends; since each bond has 2 ends, the number of distinct bonds is <span class="arithmatex">\(80/2=40\)</span>. Each bond shares 2 electrons, so the total shared electron count is <span class="arithmatex">\(40\times2=80\)</span>.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 20

Explain why amorphous silicon can still function reasonably well as a semiconductor for applications like thin-film solar cells, despite having no long-range periodic order — and identify which later chapter's core assumption this lack of order technically violates.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Amorphous silicon still preserves each atom's approximate 4-fold, tetrahedrally-coordinated local bonding environment, even though that local order does not repeat periodically over long distances. Since basic semiconducting behavior (light absorption, carrier generation) depends heavily on this short-range bonding, amorphous silicon remains usable, at some efficiency cost relative to single-crystal silicon. This lack of long-range periodicity technically violates the perfectly periodic potential that Chapter 5 assumes when solving the Schrödinger equation for an electron in a crystal — Chapter 5's energy-band theory strictly applies to single-crystal, defect-free material.</p>
</div>
</details>

### Problem 21

A hypothetical element X has 2 valence electrons and low electronegativity. Predict whether element X, bonded with itself (X–X), is more likely to form a covalent, ionic, or metallic solid, and justify your answer using the reasoning framework developed in this chapter.

<details style="margin-top: 1rem;">
<summary style="color: #5A3EED; font-weight: 700; cursor: pointer;">Show Solution</summary>
<div style="background: #E7F7E7; border: 2px solid #81C784; border-radius: 10px; padding: 18px 22px; margin-top: 10px;">
<p style="color: #333; line-height: 1.85; margin: 0;">Low electronegativity means element X does not hold its valence electrons tightly, and with no electronegativity difference between identical X atoms there is no driving force for complete electron transfer (ruling out an ionic bond). With only 2 valence electrons — too few to conveniently form the multiple shared pairs a covalent network would require, and loosely held besides — X's valence electrons are more likely to delocalize into a shared electron sea, favoring a <strong>metallic bond</strong>, similar to the alkaline-earth metals (which also have 2 valence electrons and low electronegativity).</p>
</div>
</details>
