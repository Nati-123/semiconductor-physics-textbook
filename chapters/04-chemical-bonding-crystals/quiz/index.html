    Quiz: Chemical Bonding in Semiconductor Crystals

    Test your understanding of the chemical bonding covered in Chapter 4 with these 20 questions.

    Question 1

    What is a valence electron?

    1. Any electron in an atom's nucleus
    2. An electron in an atom's outermost occupied shell, available for chemical bonding
    3. An electron that has been permanently removed from an atom
    4. A core electron tightly bound near the nucleus
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    A valence electron occupies an atom's outermost shell and is available to participate in chemical bonding. Silicon has 4 valence electrons, which determine its entire bonding behavior.

    Concept Tested: Valence Electron

    Question 2

    How many valence electrons does a neutral silicon atom have?

    1. 1
    2. 2
    3. 4
    4. 14
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: C

    Silicon's electron configuration is \(1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^2\). The 10 electrons in the first two shells are core electrons; the 4 electrons in \(3s^2\,3p^2\) are valence electrons.

    Concept Tested: Silicon Atom Structure

    Question 3

    What is silicon's full electron configuration?

    1. \(1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^2\)
    2. \(1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^1\)
    3. \(1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^5\)
    4. \(1s^2\,2s^2\)
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    Silicon (atomic number 14) has electron configuration \(1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^2\), giving it 10 core electrons and 4 valence electrons.

    Concept Tested: Silicon Atom Structure

    Question 4

    What is a covalent bond?

    1. A bond formed by transferring an electron completely from one atom to another
    2. A bond formed when two atoms each contribute one valence electron to a shared electron pair
    3. A bond formed by delocalizing electrons across an entire crystal
    4. The force holding a nucleus together
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    A covalent bond forms when two atoms each contribute one valence electron to a shared pair, attracted electrostatically to both nuclei. It is the bond type used by silicon and germanium.

    Concept Tested: Covalent Bond

    Question 5

    Why is a covalent bond described as directional?

    1. Because it points specifically along the line connecting the two specific atoms sharing the electron pair
    2. Because it pulls equally in every direction around each atom
    3. Because it has no fixed geometry at all
    4. Because it only exists in metals
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    A covalent bond's shared electron pair is localized between one specific pair of atoms, so the bond points along the line connecting those two nuclei — unlike ionic or metallic bonding, which are non-directional.

    Concept Tested: Covalent Bond

    Question 6

    What is an ionic bond?

    1. A bond formed by sharing an electron pair between two atoms
    2. A bond formed when one atom transfers one or more valence electrons completely to another, producing oppositely charged ions
    3. A bond formed only between identical atoms
    4. A bond that only exists in gases
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    An ionic bond forms when one atom transfers electrons completely to another, producing a cation and an anion held together by Coulomb attraction — non-directional, unlike a covalent bond.

    Concept Tested: Ionic Bond

    Question 7

    In the standard NaCl example, what happens to sodium's single valence electron?

    1. It is shared equally between the two atoms
    2. It delocalizes into an electron sea
    3. It is transferred completely to chlorine, producing \(\text{Na}^+\) and \(\text{Cl}^-\)
    4. It is destroyed
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: C

    Sodium's single valence electron transfers completely to chlorine, forming \(\text{Na}^+\) and \(\text{Cl}^-\), which then attract electrostatically to form the ionic bond.

    Concept Tested: Ionic Bond

    Question 8

    Which equation gives the Coulomb potential energy of an ionic bond, at ion separation \(r\)?

    1. \(U(r) = -\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\)
    2. \(U(r) = \dfrac{1}{2}kr^2\)
    3. \(U(r) = mc^2\)
    4. \(U(r) = qV\)
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    The ionic bond is simply the Coulomb potential energy between the two oppositely charged ions, \(U(r) = -e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\), from Chapter 1's electrostatics.

    Concept Tested: Ionic Bond

    Question 9

    What does the electron-sea model describe?

    1. Electrons transferred completely between two specific atoms
    2. Electrons shared between one specific pair of atoms
    3. Valence electrons delocalized across an entire crystal, surrounding fixed positive ion cores
    4. Electrons trapped permanently inside the nucleus
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: C

    In the electron-sea model of metallic bonding, valence electrons delocalize away from their parent atoms into a mobile sea surrounding fixed positive ion cores, belonging to the crystal as a whole.

    Concept Tested: Metallic Bond

    Question 10

    Which two properties does the electron-sea model of metallic bonding directly explain?

    1. Brittleness and low melting point
    2. Electrical conductivity and malleability
    3. Directional bonding and high hardness
    4. Amorphous structure and grain boundaries
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Delocalized electrons are already free to move (explaining conductivity), and the non-directional electron sea redistributes as ion cores slide past one another under stress (explaining malleability).

    Concept Tested: Metallic Bond

    Question 11

    Why does silicon bond covalently rather than metallically, despite both silicon and metals having valence electrons available for bonding?

    1. Silicon has 4 valence electrons, exactly enough to form 4 shared electron-pair bonds, rather than the loosely-bound 1-electron case that favors delocalization into an electron sea
    2. Silicon has no valence electrons at all
    3. Silicon's electrons cannot be shared under any circumstances
    4. Metals always have more protons than silicon
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    Silicon's 4 valence electrons are exactly the right number to form 4 shared electron-pair bonds, completing its octet — unlike copper's single, loosely-bound valence electron, which delocalizes readily into a metallic electron sea.

    Concept Tested: Covalent Bond

    Question 12

    What is sp3 hybridization?

    1. The transfer of electrons between two atoms
    2. The combination of one \(s\) orbital and three \(p\) orbitals into four new, equivalent hybrid orbitals
    3. The delocalization of electrons into an electron sea
    4. A type of crystal defect
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    sp3 hybridization combines one \(s\) orbital and three \(p\) orbitals into four equivalent hybrid orbitals pointing toward the corners of a regular tetrahedron.

    Concept Tested: Tetrahedral Bonding

    Question 13

    How many sp3 hybrid orbitals does silicon form from its valence orbitals?

    1. 1
    2. 2
    3. 3
    4. 4
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: D

    Silicon's one \(3s\) orbital and three \(3p\) orbitals combine into 4 equivalent sp3 hybrid orbitals, matching its 4 valence electrons and 4 covalent bonds.

    Concept Tested: Tetrahedral Bonding

    Question 14

    What is the tetrahedral bond angle formed between silicon's sp3 hybrid orbitals?

    1. \(90°\)
    2. \(109.5°\)
    3. \(120°\)
    4. \(180°\)
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    The tetrahedral bond angle is \(\theta = \arccos(-1/3) \approx 109.5°\), the angle that maximizes separation between 4 identical directions from a common center.

    Concept Tested: Tetrahedral Bonding

    Question 15

    How does tetrahedral bonding relate to the diamond lattice structure introduced in Chapter 3?

    1. It is unrelated to crystal geometry
    2. It is the chemical, first-principles explanation for the diamond lattice's coordination number of 4
    3. It explains why the diamond lattice has coordination number 12
    4. It only applies to metals
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Silicon's 4 tetrahedrally-arranged sp3 covalent bonds connect it to exactly 4 nearest neighbors, which is exactly the coordination number Chapter 3 verified geometrically for the diamond lattice structure.

    Concept Tested: Tetrahedral Bonding

    Question 16

    What is an amorphous solid?

    1. A solid with perfect long-range periodic order throughout
    2. A solid with roughly preserved local bonding but no long-range periodic order
    3. A solid made of many single-crystal grains
    4. A solid with no atoms at all
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    An amorphous solid, such as amorphous silicon, preserves roughly the same local bonding environment a crystal would prefer, but has no long-range periodic order beyond a few atomic spacings.

    Concept Tested: Amorphous Solid

    Question 17

    What is a polycrystalline solid?

    1. A single, perfectly periodic crystal with no boundaries
    2. A solid with no atoms arranged in any pattern
    3. A solid composed of many small single-crystal grains, each oriented differently, separated by grain boundaries
    4. A solid that only exists in liquids
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: C

    A polycrystalline solid, such as polysilicon, consists of many small grains, each internally a periodic single crystal, but randomly oriented relative to one another and separated by grain boundaries.

    Concept Tested: Polycrystalline Solid

    Question 18

    Why is single-crystal silicon preferred over polycrystalline silicon for the active transistor regions of an integrated circuit?

    1. Polycrystalline silicon is more expensive
    2. Grain boundaries in polycrystalline silicon scatter charge carriers and degrade electronic performance
    3. Polycrystalline silicon has no valence electrons
    4. There is no difference in performance
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Grain boundaries are disordered regions that scatter charge carriers, degrading electronic performance — which is why active transistor regions use large single crystals, while polysilicon is reserved for gate electrodes and interconnects.

    Concept Tested: Polycrystalline Solid

    Question 19

    Which of the following are the three basic types of point crystal defects?

    1. Covalent, ionic, and metallic
    2. Vacancy, interstitial, and substitutional
    3. Amorphous, polycrystalline, and single crystal
    4. Simple cubic, BCC, and FCC
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    The three basic point defects are a vacancy (a missing atom), an interstitial (an extra atom between regular sites), and a substitutional defect (a foreign atom replacing a host atom).

    Concept Tested: Crystal Defect

    Question 20

    A phosphorus atom occupies a lattice site normally held by a silicon atom. What type of defect is this, and what later chapter deliberately exploits it?

    1. A vacancy defect, exploited in Chapter 5
    2. An interstitial defect, exploited in Chapter 6
    3. A substitutional defect, exploited by doping in Chapter 8
    4. Not a defect at all
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: C

    A foreign atom (phosphorus) occupying a regular lattice site normally held by the host atom (silicon) is a substitutional defect — exactly the mechanism deliberately exploited in Chapter 8's discussion of doping.

    Concept Tested: Crystal Defect
