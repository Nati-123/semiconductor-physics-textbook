Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

Try This

  • Try this: In "Real vs. Reciprocal Lattice" view, drag the a_x slider from small to large and watch the reciprocal lattice (right panel) compress as the real lattice (left panel) expands, with the numerical readout updating live.
  • Predict: Before selecting "Rectangular lattice," predict what shape the reciprocal lattice will become if you stretch a_x much larger than a_y in real space.
  • Observe: Select "Rectangular lattice," set a_x much larger than a_y, and confirm the reciprocal lattice points become widely spaced in the x-direction but closely spaced in y — the inverse relationship applies independently along each axis.
  • Rotate: Drag the rotation slider and confirm the primitive vectors a₁/a₂, the reciprocal vectors b₁/b₂, and the shaded Brillouin zone all rotate together, since the reciprocal lattice is built directly from the real lattice.
  • Switch views: Select "Wigner-Seitz Construction" and watch the shaded first-zone rectangle resize as you adjust a_x and a_y — note it always extends exactly half the reciprocal spacing in each direction.
  • Explain: Explain why the dashed perpendicular-bisector lines, not the reciprocal lattice points themselves, are what actually define the Brillouin zone boundary.

Connects to Chapter 5: This MicroSim visualizes the reciprocal lattice (spacing \(2\pi/a\) along each axis) constructed from a real-space lattice (Chapter 3), and the first Brillouin zone as the Wigner-Seitz primitive cell of that reciprocal lattice — the same k-space region whose boundary, \(k=\pm\pi/a\) in 1D, is exactly where the Kronig-Penney model's band gaps open.

What Each Control Does

View dropdown
Switches between "Real vs. Reciprocal Lattice" (side-by-side comparison, with the first Brillouin zone overlaid) and "Wigner-Seitz Construction" (zoomed construction of the first Brillouin zone).
Shape dropdown
"Square lattice" locks a_y to always match a_x; "Rectangular lattice" allows a_x and a_y to be set independently.
a_x, a_y sliders
Set the real-space lattice constants (in nanometers); the reciprocal lattice spacing updates as \(2\pi/a\) along each axis, shown numerically below the controls.
Rotation slider
Rotates the real lattice, its primitive vectors, and the corresponding reciprocal lattice/Brillouin zone together, by the same angle.

Challenge Questions

1. As the real-space lattice constant a_x increases, what happens to the reciprocal lattice spacing along x?

Reciprocal lattice spacing is \(2\pi/a\), so a larger real-space lattice constant produces a smaller (more closely spaced) reciprocal lattice — an inverse relationship.

Drag the a_x slider to its largest value and compare the reciprocal-lattice spacing in the right panel to its value at the smallest a_x.

2. What construction defines the boundary of the first Brillouin zone shown in "Wigner-Seitz Construction" view?

The Brillouin zone is the Wigner-Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice — the region enclosed by perpendicular bisector planes to the nearest reciprocal lattice points, shown as dashed lines in this MicroSim.

Switch to "Wigner-Seitz Construction" view and look at the dashed lines connecting to the shaded rectangle.

3. If a_x is stretched much larger than a_y in real space, what shape does the Brillouin zone become?

Because reciprocal spacing is inversely proportional to real-space spacing along each axis independently, stretching a_x (making it large) shrinks the reciprocal spacing along k_x, making the Brillouin zone narrow in k_x and, by comparison, tall in k_y.

Select "Rectangular lattice," set a_x much larger than a_y, and switch to "Wigner-Seitz Construction" view to see the resulting rectangle's proportions.

4. When you drag the rotation slider, what happens to the reciprocal lattice and Brillouin zone?

The reciprocal lattice vectors are defined directly from the real-space lattice vectors, so rotating the real lattice rotates the reciprocal lattice — and the Brillouin zone built from it — by exactly the same angle.

Drag the rotation slider and watch both panels together, including the a₁/a₂ and b₁/b₂ vectors.
