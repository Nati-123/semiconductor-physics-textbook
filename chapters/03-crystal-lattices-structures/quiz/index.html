    Quiz: Crystal Lattices and Structures

    Test your understanding of the crystal lattices and structures covered in Chapter 3 with these 20 questions.

    Question 1

    What is a crystal lattice?

    1. A single atom bonded to its nearest neighbors
    2. An infinite, perfectly periodic array of points in space, with no atoms attached
    3. Any solid material with a fixed volume
    4. The chemical formula of a compound
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    A crystal lattice is a purely geometric idealization: an infinite, perfectly periodic array of points, with every point having an environment identical to every other point. Attaching a basis of atoms to each point produces a crystal structure.

    Concept Tested: Crystal Lattice

    Question 2

    A crystal structure is formed by:

    1. Removing all atoms from a lattice
    2. Attaching a basis (one or more atoms) to every point of a lattice
    3. Melting a crystal and letting it cool
    4. Doubling the lattice constant
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Crystal Structure = Lattice + Basis. Attaching one or more atoms (the basis) to every point of the lattice produces the actual physical crystal structure.

    Concept Tested: Crystal Lattice

    Question 3

    What is a unit cell?

    1. A single isolated atom
    2. A repeating volume that tiles all of space by translation to reproduce the entire lattice
    3. The energy required to remove an electron from an atom
    4. A measure of electrical conductivity
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    A unit cell is a repeating volume that, when tiled by simple translation, reproduces the full lattice. The conventional choice for a cubic crystal is a cube with edge length equal to the lattice constant.

    Concept Tested: Unit Cell

    Question 4

    How many lattice points does a primitive cell contain, by definition?

    1. Exactly one
    2. Exactly two
    3. Exactly four
    4. As many as the conventional cell
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    A primitive cell is, by definition, the smallest possible unit cell, containing exactly one lattice point when shared points are counted by the fraction actually inside the cell.

    Concept Tested: Primitive Cell

    Question 5

    What physically does the lattice constant \(a\) represent for a cubic crystal?

    1. The number of atoms in the crystal
    2. The edge length of the conventional cubic unit cell
    3. The coordination number
    4. The packing fraction
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    The lattice constant \(a\) is the edge length of the conventional cubic unit cell, setting the fundamental length scale of the crystal. Silicon, for example, has \(a=0.543\) nm.

    Concept Tested: Lattice Constant

    Question 6

    How many atoms are contained in the conventional unit cell of the simple cubic (SC) structure?

    1. 1
    2. 2
    3. 4
    4. 8
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    The 8 corner atoms are each shared among 8 cells, contributing \(8\times\tfrac18=1\) atom per cell.

    Concept Tested: Simple Cubic Structure

    Question 7

    What is the coordination number of the simple cubic structure?

    1. 4
    2. 6
    3. 8
    4. 12
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Simple cubic has coordination number 6 — one nearest neighbor along each of the \(\pm x\), \(\pm y\), and \(\pm z\) directions.

    Concept Tested: Simple Cubic Structure

    Question 8

    How many atoms per conventional unit cell does the body-centered cubic (BCC) structure contain?

    1. 1
    2. 2
    3. 4
    4. 8
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    The 8 corner atoms contribute \(8\times\tfrac18=1\) atom, and the single whole body-center atom contributes 1 more, for a total of 2 atoms per cell.

    Concept Tested: Body-Centered Cubic

    Question 9

    In the body-centered cubic structure, along which direction do atoms touch?

    1. The cube edge
    2. The face diagonal
    3. The body diagonal
    4. Atoms never touch in BCC
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: C

    In BCC, corner atoms touch the body-center atom along the cube's body diagonal, giving the touching condition \(4r=\sqrt3\,a\).

    Concept Tested: Body-Centered Cubic

    Question 10

    How many atoms per conventional unit cell does the face-centered cubic (FCC) structure contain?

    1. 1
    2. 2
    3. 4
    4. 8
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: C

    Corner atoms contribute \(8\times\tfrac18=1\) atom, and 6 face atoms, each shared between 2 cells, contribute \(6\times\tfrac12=3\) atoms, for a total of 4 atoms per cell.

    Concept Tested: Face-Centered Cubic

    Question 11

    Which of the three cubic Bravais lattices has the highest packing fraction?

    1. Simple cubic (SC)
    2. Body-centered cubic (BCC)
    3. Face-centered cubic (FCC)
    4. All three have identical packing fractions
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: C

    FCC has the highest packing fraction, approximately 0.740, compared to 0.524 for SC and 0.680 for BCC, consistent with its highest coordination number of 12.

    Concept Tested: Face-Centered Cubic

    Question 12

    The diamond lattice structure is best described as:

    1. A single simple cubic lattice
    2. Two interpenetrating FCC lattices offset by \((1/4,1/4,1/4)a\) along the body diagonal
    3. A body-centered cubic lattice with no basis
    4. A random, non-periodic arrangement of atoms
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Diamond cubic consists of two interpenetrating FCC lattices, the second shifted relative to the first by \((1/4,1/4,1/4)a\) along the body diagonal, giving 8 atoms per conventional cell.

    Concept Tested: Diamond Lattice Structure

    Question 13

    Why is the coordination number of the diamond lattice structure 4 rather than 12?

    1. Because diamond is not periodic
    2. Because the large \((1/4,1/4,1/4)a\) offset between the two interpenetrating FCC sublattices makes each atom's nearest neighbors the four closest atoms on the other sublattice, not the twelve neighbors within its own sublattice
    3. Because diamond has no basis
    4. Because diamond atoms carry no charge
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    The offset between the two FCC sublattices is much larger than the spacing between nearest neighbors within a single FCC lattice, so each atom's true nearest neighbors are the four closest atoms on the other, shifted sublattice — giving tetrahedral, 4-fold coordination rather than the 12-fold coordination of a simple FCC lattice.

    Concept Tested: Diamond Lattice Structure

    Question 14

    What distinguishes the zincblende structure from the diamond structure?

    1. Zincblende has a different lattice geometry entirely
    2. Zincblende places two different atomic species on the two interpenetrating sublattices, whereas diamond has the same species on both
    3. Zincblende has no tetrahedral coordination
    4. Zincblende has more atoms per conventional cell than diamond
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Zincblende is geometrically identical to diamond — same two interpenetrating FCC sublattices, same 8 atoms per cell, same tetrahedral coordination — but with two different atomic species occupying the two sublattices, as in gallium and arsenic in GaAs.

    Concept Tested: Zincblende Structure

    Question 15

    Why does the zincblende structure lack the inversion symmetry present in the diamond structure?

    1. Because inverting through an atom would map its neighbors onto positions occupied by a different, non-interchangeable atomic species
    2. Because zincblende crystals are not periodic
    3. Because zincblende has a smaller lattice constant
    4. Because zincblende atoms are not tetrahedrally coordinated
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    In diamond, every atom is chemically identical, so inversion maps the crystal onto itself. In zincblende, the two sublattices hold different species, so inverting through one atom maps its neighbors onto positions belonging to the other species — the two are not interchangeable, breaking inversion symmetry.

    Concept Tested: Zincblende Structure

    Question 16

    What is the correct procedure for finding the Miller indices of a crystal plane?

    1. Multiply the axis intercepts directly with no further steps
    2. Find the axis intercepts (in units of the lattice constant), take their reciprocals, then clear fractions to the smallest integers
    3. Add the axis intercepts together
    4. Take the square root of each axis intercept
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Miller indices are found by taking the axis intercepts in units of the lattice constant, computing their reciprocals, and then clearing any fractions by multiplying through by the smallest common integer.

    Concept Tested: Miller Indices

    Question 17

    A crystal plane intercepts the \(x\) axis at \(2a\), the \(y\) axis at \(1a\), and never intercepts the \(z\) axis. What are its Miller indices?

    1. \((210)\)
    2. \((120)\)
    3. \((211)\)
    4. \((021)\)
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Reciprocals of the intercepts \(2,\ 1,\ \infty\) are \(1/2,\ 1,\ 0\). Clearing the fraction by multiplying through by 2 gives \(1,\ 2,\ 0\), so the Miller indices are \((120)\).

    Concept Tested: Miller Indices

    Question 18

    What does a Miller index of 0 in a given position mean?

    1. The plane passes through the origin only
    2. The plane is parallel to that axis (it never intersects it)
    3. The crystal has no atoms along that axis
    4. The lattice constant along that axis is zero
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    A Miller index of 0 arises from an intercept at infinity, since \(1/\infty = 0\). Physically, this means the plane runs parallel to that axis and never crosses it.

    Concept Tested: Miller Indices

    Question 19

    Which family of crystal planes is most commonly used to cut silicon wafers for integrated-circuit fabrication?

    1. \((100)\)
    2. \((999)\)
    3. \((010)\) only, never any other plane
    4. Crystal planes have no relevance to wafer cutting
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    Silicon wafers are most commonly cut along the \((100)\) plane, which offers a favorable, low-defect interface for growing the oxide layers used in transistor gates, though \((111)\)-oriented wafers are also used for certain applications.

    Concept Tested: Crystal Plane

    Question 20

    Why do diamond-cubic crystals such as silicon and germanium cleave preferentially along \((111)\) planes?

    1. Because \((111)\) planes have the highest atomic density and the weakest bonding between adjacent planes
    2. Because \((111)\) planes contain no atoms at all
    3. Because \((111)\) is the only possible Miller index for a cubic crystal
    4. Because cleaving is unrelated to crystal plane orientation
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    \((111)\) planes in diamond-cubic crystals have the highest atomic density and the weakest bonding between adjacent planes, making them the planes of preferential cleavage — a fact directly exploited during wafer dicing in semiconductor manufacturing.

    Concept Tested: Crystal Plane
