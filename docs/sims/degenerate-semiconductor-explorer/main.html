What to Observe

  • The band diagram shows EC, the intrinsic level Ei (dashed, midgap), and EV, with a shaded orange "near-degenerate" zone within 3kBT of the relevant band edge.
  • The colored EF line moves as you change doping: green when non-degenerate, orange approaching degenerate, and red-dashed ("estimate") once the simple formula predicts EF has reached or entered the band.
  • The doping gauge on the right shows the same three zones on a log doping-concentration scale, with live boundaries that shift when you change temperature.

Guided Exploration

  • Try this: Start at the default ND = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ and confirm the badge reads NON-DEGENERATE, with EF comfortably inside the gap, well below EC.
  • Predict: Before dragging ND up past 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, predict whether EF will move toward EC or EV.
  • Observe: Continuously increase ND from 10¹⁴ to 10²¹ cm⁻³ and watch EF rise steadily from deep in the gap, through the orange transition zone, and into the shaded conduction-band region -- read the badge and explanation text as each zone change happens.
  • Explore: Raise the temperature slider and watch the zone boundaries on the gauge shift -- NC(T) grows with T, so a higher temperature needs slightly heavier doping to reach the same degree of degeneracy.
  • Switch type: Change to p-type and repeat with NA, watching EF approach and enter EV instead.

Connects to Chapter 8: This MicroSim computes \(E_C-E_F=k_BT\ln(N_C(T)/N_D)\) (n-type) or \(E_F-E_V=k_BT\ln(N_V(T)/N_A)\) (p-type), the same relationship this chapter's "Degenerate Semiconductors" section previews. This formula assumes non-degenerate, Boltzmann-like statistics -- once doping is heavy enough that the formula itself predicts E_F at or past a band edge, the formula is no longer a valid description of the real carrier statistics, and the diagram switches to a dashed, explicitly-labeled "estimate" rather than implying false precision. The exact treatment, using the full Fermi-Dirac integral, is Chapter 9's subject.

What Each Control Does

Type dropdown
Switches between n-type (EF approaches EC) and p-type (EF approaches EV).
ND / NA slider
Sets the doping concentration continuously from very light (10¹⁴ cm⁻³) to extremely heavy (10²¹ cm⁻³), sweeping through all three zones.
Temperature slider
Sets T, which changes both kBT and the temperature-dependent effective density of states NC(T) or NV(T) -- both zone boundaries move accordingly.

Challenge Questions

1. Silicon is doped at N_D = 5×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ at 300 K, where N_C ≈ 2.8×10¹⁹ cm⁻³. Is this sample degenerate?

Since ND (5×10¹⁹) exceeds NC (2.8×10¹⁹), ln(NC/ND) is negative, so the formula predicts EC − EF ≤ 0 -- EF has reached or moved past EC. Set the slider to 19.7 and confirm the badge reads DEGENERATE.

Set the ND slider to 5×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ (exponent 19.7) at T = 300 K and read the badge.

2. Once the badge reads DEGENERATE, why does the diagram switch EF to a dashed "estimate" line instead of a solid precise one?

The formula EC-EF = kT ln(NC/ND) is derived assuming Boltzmann (non-degenerate) statistics. Once doping pushes the predicted EF at or past the band edge, that assumption is violated, so the computed value is only a rough estimate -- the exact position requires the full Fermi-Dirac integral, covered in Chapter 9.

Re-read the explanation text that appears below the badge once you reach the DEGENERATE zone.

3. If you raise the temperature while keeping ND fixed in the transition zone, what happens to NC(T), and how does that affect the zone boundaries on the gauge?

NC(T) grows as T^1.5, so raising T increases the effective density of states near EC, pushing the non-degenerate/transition and transition/degenerate boundaries on the gauge toward higher doping values.

Drag the temperature slider up and watch the green/orange/red zone boundaries on the doping gauge shift right.

Key Takeaway: "Degenerate" is not a vague label -- it marks the specific point where the Fermi level reaches a band edge and the standard non-degenerate (Boltzmann) approximation this entire chapter relies on stops being valid, requiring the full Fermi-Dirac treatment instead. Heavy doping is used deliberately in real devices (like transistor contacts) precisely because low resistivity matters more there than the validity of simplified statistics.
