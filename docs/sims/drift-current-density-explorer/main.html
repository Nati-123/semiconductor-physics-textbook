What to Observe

  • The bar chart compares J_n and J_p on a log scale — in extrinsic material one bar is dramatically taller, since the minority carrier's concentration (from Chapter 9's mass action law) is orders of magnitude smaller.
  • The info panel shows every factor in the chain — n₀, p₀, μ_n, μ_p — before combining them into J_n, J_p, and J_total, all in real A/cm² units.
  • The green message at the bottom states, in words, which carrier dominates and by what factor.

Guided Exploration

  • Try this: Start with the "n-type" preset and note how many orders of magnitude larger J_n is than J_p.
  • Predict: Before switching to the "Intrinsic" preset, predict whether J_n and J_p will become equal, and whether J_total will grow or shrink.
  • Observe: Switch to "Intrinsic" and confirm J_n and J_p are now comparable (both set by the same nᵢ), but J_total is far smaller than in the doped presets — intrinsic material conducts poorly.
  • Explore: Raise the electric field slider and confirm every current density scales up proportionally — E is the one factor in J=qnμE that does not depend on doping or material at all.

Connects to Chapter 11: Drift current density is \(J=qn\mu E\) for electrons (and the analogous \(qp\mu_pE\) for holes), combining Chapter 9-10's equilibrium carrier concentration with this chapter's mobility. In any extrinsic (doped) sample, the mass action law (Chapter 9) forces the minority carrier concentration far below the majority carrier's, so — even though electrons and holes can have comparably-sized mobilities — the minority carrier's contribution to total drift current is almost always negligible. This is exactly why Chapter 11's own Worked Example 1 could safely ignore the hole contribution when computing drift current for an n-type sample.

What Each Control Does

Preset dropdown
Jumps directly to intrinsic, n-type, or p-type doping; switches to "Custom" once you move a doping slider.
Material dropdown
Selects the effective masses and band gap used to compute nᵢ(T).
Temperature slider
Sets T, affecting both nᵢ(T) and the Matthiessen's-rule mobility.
N_D and N_A sliders
Set donor and acceptor concentrations, determining n₀ and p₀ via the exact carrier concentration equation.
Field E slider
Sets the applied electric field strength (log scale), which multiplies every current density equally.

Challenge Questions

1. Predict first: in an n-type sample with N_D far above N_A, which term dominates J_total?

Even though μ_p is somewhat smaller than μ_n, the concentration difference (n₀≫p₀ in n-type material) is many more orders of magnitude than the mobility difference, so J_n dominates overwhelmingly.

Select the "n-type" preset and compare the bar heights.

2. Why does J_total stay relatively small for the "Intrinsic" preset even though J_n and J_p are now comparable to each other?

Intrinsic carrier concentration nᵢ is typically many orders of magnitude smaller than a doped sample's majority carrier concentration, so even with both carriers contributing, the total current density is small — this is exactly why doping is used to make semiconductors useful conductors.

Compare nᵢ's value (shown for n₀=p₀ in the Intrinsic preset) to N_D in the n-type preset.

Key Takeaway: J=qnμE has three factors, but in real doped semiconductors one of them (carrier concentration) varies over far more orders of magnitude than the other two (mobility, field) combined — which is why the majority-carrier term almost always dominates drift current, and why "doping" is the primary tool device engineers use to control conductivity.
