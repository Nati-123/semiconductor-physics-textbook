Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

Try This

  • Try this: Set m*/m₀ = 0.26 and εr = 11.7 (silicon's values) and read the computed ionization energy — compare it to the real measured value of about 0.045 eV.
  • Predict: Before increasing εr, predict whether the ionization energy will go up or down.
  • Observe: Increase εr and watch both the ionization energy shrink and the orbit radius grow — stronger dielectric screening spreads the electron farther from the ion and binds it more weakly.

Connects to Chapter 7: A donor's loosely-bound fifth electron (or an acceptor's hole) can be modeled as a hydrogen-like system: the electron orbits the fixed ion core just as in Chapter 2's hydrogen-atom picture, but with two crucial changes — the electron's mass is replaced by the semiconductor's effective mass \(m^*\), and the Coulomb attraction is weakened by the crystal's relative dielectric constant \(\varepsilon_r\), which screens the ion's charge. Both effects combine to make the ionization energy roughly a thousand times smaller than hydrogen's 13.6 eV, exactly matching the tiny (tens of meV) donor and acceptor ionization energies measured in real semiconductors.

What Each Control Does

Effective mass ratio slider (m*/m₀)
Sets the effective mass used in the hydrogenic ionization-energy formula.
Relative dielectric constant slider (εr)
Sets the screening strength of the host crystal.

Challenge Questions

1. Why is a donor's ionization energy so much smaller than hydrogen's 13.6 eV?

The hydrogenic ionization energy scales as (m*/m0)/εr²; both a reduced effective mass and, especially, a large dielectric constant (squared in the denominator) shrink the ionization energy dramatically compared to a bare hydrogen atom.
