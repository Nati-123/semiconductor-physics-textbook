What to Observe

  • Two visual layers are deliberately kept separate: square symbols are fixed ionized dopants (acceptors on the p-side, donors on the n-side) — they are drawn at the same position on every step. Round symbols are mobile carriers (holes, electrons) — these are the ones that diffuse, recombine, and disappear near the junction as the steps advance.
  • The fixed ions never change between Step 1 and Step 8 — only the carrier layer thins out near the junction, which is the entire physical origin of the depletion region.
  • Steps 6-8 mark the depletion-region boundaries \(-x_p\) and \(x_n\) directly on the diagram, and Step 7-8 add the built-in field arrow, pointing from the n-side toward the p-side.

Connects to Chapter 14: Joining a p-type and n-type region at a metallurgical junction forms a p-n junction. Diffusion across the junction, followed by recombination, sweeps mobile carriers out of a thin region on either side, creating the depletion region. The depletion approximation idealizes this region as fully depleted with abrupt edges — the starting point for every equation derived later in the chapter.

What Each Step Shows

Steps 1-2
Separate p-type and n-type regions, each individually neutral, are joined at the metallurgical junction.
Steps 3-4
Majority carriers diffuse across the junction (Step 3), then recombine as minority carriers near the interface (Step 4).
Steps 5-6
The fixed dopant ions left behind are now uncovered/exposed (Step 5), forming the depletion region with sharp boundaries \(-x_p\) and \(x_n\) (Step 6).
Steps 7-8
The exposed charge creates a built-in electric field (Step 7) that grows until it exactly balances diffusion, reaching equilibrium (Step 8).

Challenge Questions

1. What is left behind inside the depletion region after mobile carriers are swept out?

Correct. The ionized acceptors and donors are fixed in the crystal lattice and cannot diffuse away — only the mobile carriers that once neutralized them are gone.

Think about which particles in the crystal can move (diffuse and recombine) and which are locked in place.

2. Why does the built-in electric field eventually stop further diffusion, bringing the junction to equilibrium?

Correct. Equilibrium is reached precisely when drift current equals diffusion current everywhere, so the net current is zero — this is the same drift-diffusion balance from Chapters 11-12, now applied at a junction.

Recall how drift and diffusion current were shown to combine in Chapters 11-12 — what condition defines equilibrium?

3. In Step 1, why are BOTH square (ion) and round (carrier) symbols shown in each region, in roughly equal numbers?

Each neutral region has \(p\approx N_A\) (p-side) or \(n\approx N_D\) (n-side): one mobile carrier for every fixed ion. That 1:1 balance is exactly what gets disrupted near the junction in later steps, uncovering net charge.

Compare a neutral region (Step 1) to the depletion region (Step 6) — what's different about the ratio of square symbols to round symbols?

Key Takeaway: The depletion region is not a new kind of matter appearing at the junction — it is an absence. Fixed ionized dopants never move; only the mobile carriers that used to neutralize them diffuse away and recombine. What's left behind is exposed, uncompensated fixed charge, and it is precisely that charge — via Poisson's equation, covered next in this chapter — that creates the built-in electric field holding the junction at equilibrium.
