Try This

  • Try this: Keep the doping type fixed at n-type and cycle through all four metals, noting which ones classify as rectifying and which as ohmic.
  • Predict: Before switching doping type from n-type to p-type with the same metal, predict whether the classification will flip.
  • Observe: Whenever the contact is rectifying, the Schottky I-V curve sits far above the p-n reference curve at low forward voltage — a direct visual of the "lower turn-on voltage" advantage.

Connects to Chapter 16: A metal-semiconductor junction is a rectifying contact (forming a Schottky barrier) or an ohmic contact depending on how Φ_M compares to Φ_S — and the rule flips between n-type and p-type material. When rectifying, the junction behaves as a Schottky diode whose thermionic-emission current typically dwarfs an equivalent p-n diode's at the same forward voltage.

What Each Control Does

Metal
Sets Φ_M, compared against Φ_S to classify the contact.
Doping type
n-type or p-type — the classification rule (which side of Φ_M vs Φ_S is rectifying) flips between them.
N
Sets doping concentration, which sets Φ_S.

Challenge Questions

1. On n-type material, which condition produces a rectifying contact?

Correct. On n-type material, Φ_M > Φ_S pulls electrons out of the semiconductor at contact, forming a depleted, rectifying Schottky barrier.

Try setting the sim to n-type and switching metals — which condition (shown in the classification card) lines up with "RECTIFYING"?

2. Why does the Schottky diode curve sit above the p-n diode curve at the same forward voltage?

Correct. Thermionic emission over a barrier is far more efficient than minority-carrier diffusion across a quasi-neutral region, giving Schottky diodes a saturation current many orders of magnitude larger.

Compare the J0 values printed under each curve — which one is larger, and what does that do to the whole exponential curve?
