Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

What to Observe

  • The card on the left shows N_C and N_V computed directly from the formula shown (not a lookup table), alongside the live band gap E_g(T) and intrinsic concentration n_i.
  • N_C is not a literal count of electron states — it is a single number that collapses the entire conduction-band density-of-states integral into "as if all states sat exactly at E_C with degeneracy N_C."
  • Switch the View dropdown to see three different plots of the same underlying physics: n_i(T) for one material, N_C(T) and N_V(T) together, or an n_i(T) comparison across all three materials.

Guided Exploration

  • Try this: Select "Silicon" at 300 K and compare the computed N_C and n_i to the commonly quoted textbook values (N_C≈2.8×10^19 cm^-3, n_i≈10^10 cm^-3).
  • Predict: Before switching to GaAs, predict whether N_C will be larger or smaller than silicon's, given GaAs's much smaller electron effective mass.
  • Observe: Drag the temperature slider and watch n_i change by orders of magnitude for only a modest change in T — the exponential sensitivity flagged all the way back in Chapter 1.
  • Compare: Switch View to "N_C(T) & N_V(T) — power law" and confirm both curves rise gently (T^1.5); then switch to the n_i(T) view for the same material and notice how much more steeply that curve rises — the exponential term dominates.
  • Explore: Switch View to "Compare nᵢ(T)" and confirm GaAs's curve sits consistently below silicon's and germanium's at every temperature.

Connects to Chapter 9: The effective density of states, \(N_C=2(2\pi m_e^*k_BT/h^2)^{3/2}\) (and the analogous \(N_V\) for holes), collapses the entire conduction-band density-of-states integral into a single number, letting the non-degenerate carrier concentration be written simply as \(n_0=N_Ce^{-(E_C-E_F)/k_BT}\). Combining \(N_C\) and \(N_V\) with the band gap gives the intrinsic carrier concentration, \(n_i=\sqrt{N_CN_V}\,e^{-E_g/2k_BT}\) — the single number that anchors every carrier-concentration calculation in this and later chapters.

What Each Control Does

Material dropdown
Selects the density-of-states effective masses and Varshni band-gap constants used in the calculation.
Temperature slider
Sets T, recomputing N_C, N_V, E_g(T), and n_i(T) live.
View dropdown
Switches the chart between a single material's n_i(T), N_C(T)/N_V(T) together, or an n_i(T) comparison across Si, Ge, and GaAs.

Challenge Questions

1. Why does GaAs have a much smaller N_C than silicon?

N_C scales as (m*)^(3/2), and GaAs's much smaller electron effective mass (0.067 m0 vs. silicon's 1.08 m0) directly produces a much smaller N_C.

Compare the electron effective mass values shown for silicon and GaAs.

2. In the "N_C(T) & N_V(T)" view, why do both curves rise so much more gently than n_i(T) does in the other view?

n_i = √(N_C·N_V)·e^(−Eg/2kT) contains N_C and N_V's T^1.5 dependence AND the exponential e^(−Eg/2kT) term — and the exponential term dominates n_i's overall temperature sensitivity by far.

Compare the two view modes at the same temperature range and look at how many orders of magnitude each curve spans.

Key Takeaway: N_C and N_V are bookkeeping constants, not physical state counts — they let a hard integral become simple algebra. Because n_i depends on N_C and N_V only through a mild T^1.5 power law but on the band gap through a steep exponential, band gap (not effective mass) is what dominates how dramatically n_i differs between materials and across temperature.
