    Quiz: Drift Current and Carrier Mobility

    Test your understanding of drift current and carrier mobility covered in Chapter 11 with these 20 questions.

    Question 1

    What is drift velocity?

    1. A carrier's total random thermal speed
    2. The small, steady net velocity added to random motion by an applied field
    3. The speed of light in the material
    4. A carrier's charge divided by its mass
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Drift velocity is the small, steady net velocity an applied field superimposes on a carrier's much larger random thermal motion.

    Concept Tested: Drift Velocity

    Question 2

    What produces drift current?

    1. A carrier concentration gradient
    2. An applied electric field
    3. A temperature gradient
    4. A change in band gap
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Drift current is produced by an applied electric field biasing carriers' random motion into a net drift.

    Concept Tested: Drift Current

    Question 3

    What produces diffusion current?

    1. An applied electric field only
    2. A carrier concentration gradient
    3. A magnetic field
    4. The band gap alone
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Diffusion current is driven by a carrier concentration gradient, independent of any applied field.

    Concept Tested: Diffusion Current

    Question 4

    What does carrier mobility measure?

    1. Carrier charge
    2. How efficiently an applied field converts into net drift velocity
    3. The band gap energy
    4. The number of carriers present
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Mobility (v_d/E) measures how efficiently an applied field converts into net drift, given the scattering environment.

    Concept Tested: Carrier Mobility

    Question 5

    What causes lattice scattering?

    1. Ionized dopant atoms
    2. Thermally-vibrating lattice atoms
    3. The Fermi level
    4. Photon absorption
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Lattice scattering is collision with the thermally-vibrating atoms of the crystal lattice.

    Concept Tested: Lattice Scattering

    Question 6

    What causes impurity scattering?

    1. Lattice vibrations
    2. The Coulomb field of ionized donor or acceptor atoms
    3. Photon emission
    4. Band gap narrowing
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Impurity scattering is collision with the Coulomb field of ionized dopant atoms.

    Concept Tested: Impurity Scattering

    Question 7

    According to Matthiessen's rule, how are multiple scattering mechanisms combined?

    1. Their mobilities are added directly
    2. Their reciprocal mobilities (scattering rates) are added
    3. Only the largest mobility is used
    4. They are averaged
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Matthiessen's rule adds reciprocal mobilities: 1/μ = 1/μ_L + 1/μ_I, like resistors in parallel.

    Concept Tested: Scattering Mechanism

    Question 8

    Which scattering mechanism typically dominates at low temperature and heavy doping?

    1. Lattice scattering
    2. Impurity scattering
    3. Neither dominates ever
    4. Photon scattering
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Impurity scattering dominates at low temperature (slow carriers deflected more) and heavy doping (more impurities present).

    Concept Tested: Impurity Scattering

    Question 9

    State the conductivity formula.

    1. \(\sigma = q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)\)
    2. \(\sigma = n+p\)
    3. \(\sigma = \mu_n-\mu_p\)
    4. \(\sigma = R_s\times(L/W)\)
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    Conductivity combines carrier concentration and mobility for both carrier types: σ = q(nμn+pμp).

    Concept Tested: Conductivity

    Question 10

    What is resistivity's relationship to conductivity?

    1. Resistivity equals conductivity
    2. Resistivity is the reciprocal of conductivity
    3. Resistivity is conductivity squared
    4. They are unrelated
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Resistivity ρ = 1/σ, the reciprocal of conductivity.

    Concept Tested: Resistivity

    Question 11

    What is sheet resistance, and what are its typical units?

    1. The thin-film form of resistivity, ρ/t, in ohms per square
    2. The same thing as resistivity, in ohm-cm
    3. A measure of carrier concentration
    4. Mobility divided by charge
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    Sheet resistance, Rs=ρ/t, is measured in ohms per square (Ω/□).

    Concept Tested: Sheet Resistance

    Question 12

    A resistor's length and width are both doubled on the same film. What happens to its resistance?

    1. It doubles
    2. It stays the same
    3. It quadruples
    4. It halves
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Since R = Rs×(L/W), doubling both L and W leaves the ratio (number of squares) unchanged, so resistance stays the same.

    Concept Tested: Sheet Resistance

    Question 13

    Why do electron and hole drift currents add rather than cancel in the same applied field?

    1. They actually do cancel
    2. Opposite charges drift in opposite directions, but current direction is defined by positive charge flow, so both contributions point the same way
    3. Holes do not respond to electric fields
    4. Electrons and holes always have equal concentration
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Electrons and holes drift in opposite physical directions, but since current is defined by positive-charge-equivalent flow direction, both contributions add.

    Concept Tested: Drift Current

    Question 14

    What does the Einstein relation connect, as previewed in this chapter for Chapter 12?

    1. Diffusion coefficient and mobility
    2. Band gap and lattice constant
    3. Sheet resistance and thickness only
    4. Two unrelated quantities with no physical connection
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    The Einstein relation, developed fully in Chapter 12, connects the diffusion coefficient D to mobility μ, since both are set by the same scattering physics.

    Concept Tested: Diffusion Current

    Question 15

    Why does conductivity generally still increase with doping, even though mobility decreases from impurity scattering?

    1. Mobility's decrease always outweighs the concentration increase
    2. Carrier concentration increases much faster (many orders of magnitude) than mobility decreases
    3. Conductivity does not depend on either quantity
    4. Doping has no effect on conductivity
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Doping can raise carrier concentration by many orders of magnitude, while mobility typically drops by only a modest factor, so conductivity (their product) still rises overall.

    Concept Tested: Conductivity

    Question 16

    In an extrinsic (heavily n-type doped) sample, which carrier's contribution to conductivity is negligible?

    1. Electrons (majority carrier)
    2. Holes (minority carrier)
    3. Both contribute equally
    4. Neither contributes
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    The minority carrier (holes, in n-type material) is suppressed by the mass action law and contributes negligibly to conductivity.

    Concept Tested: Conductivity

    Question 17

    Why is resistivity commonly measured directly with a four-point probe rather than computed from carrier concentration and mobility?

    1. Because resistivity cannot be computed from those quantities at all
    2. Because it is a directly measurable macroscopic quantity, useful without separately knowing n and μ
    3. Because four-point probes measure band gap instead
    4. Because resistivity is unrelated to conductivity
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Resistivity can be measured directly and used practically without needing separate knowledge of carrier concentration and mobility individually.

    Concept Tested: Resistivity

    Question 18

    If a combined mobility from Matthiessen's rule is computed from μ_L=1000 and μ_I=1000 (equal), is the combined mobility larger, smaller, or equal to each individual value?

    1. Larger than 1000
    2. Smaller than 1000 (specifically 500)
    3. Exactly 1000
    4. Exactly 2000
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    1/μ = 1/1000+1/1000 = 2/1000, so μ=500 — always smaller than either individual mobility, since reciprocals are added.

    Concept Tested: Scattering Mechanism

    Question 19

    A film's thickness is cut in half, with resistivity unchanged. What happens to its sheet resistance?

    1. It doubles
    2. It is cut in half
    3. It stays the same
    4. It becomes zero
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    Since Rs=ρ/t, halving t doubles Rs for the same resistivity.

    Concept Tested: Sheet Resistance

    Question 20

    Which chapter develops diffusion current and the Einstein relation fully, building on this chapter's introduction?

    1. Chapter 12
    2. Chapter 1
    3. Chapter 6
    4. No later chapter builds on this
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    Chapter 12, "Diffusion and Advanced Transport Phenomena," develops diffusion current, the Einstein relation, and the Hall effect fully.

    Concept Tested: Diffusion Current
