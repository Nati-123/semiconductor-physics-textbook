Try This

  • Try this: Select "Boron (B)" and count the acceptor atom's bonds — only 3 are complete, since boron has just 3 valence electrons.
  • Observe: Notice the incomplete 4th bond, shown as a hole (open red circle) right next to the acceptor atom.
  • Click "Ionize Acceptor" and watch the hole move away (an electron from a neighboring bond fills the vacancy instead), while the acceptor atom becomes a fixed, negatively-charged ion.
  • Try this: Switch the dopant dropdown between Boron, Aluminum, Gallium, and Indium and confirm all four still create exactly one hole.

Connects to Chapter 7: An acceptor atom is a Group III element (3 valence electrons) substituted onto a silicon lattice site. It can only complete 3 of its 4 required covalent bonds, leaving one bond incomplete — a hole. At ordinary temperatures, an electron from a neighboring bond easily hops into this vacancy, effectively moving the hole away through the lattice and leaving the acceptor atom as a fixed, immobile negative ion — without needing to break any covalent bond elsewhere first.

What Each Control Does

Dopant dropdown
Selects which Group III acceptor atom (B, Al, Ga, or In) occupies the substitutional lattice site.
Ionize Acceptor button
Toggles the hole between sitting at the acceptor site and having moved away into the lattice.

Challenge Questions

1. How many covalent bonds can a Group III acceptor atom complete on its own?

A Group III atom has only 3 valence electrons, enough to complete only 3 of the 4 covalent bonds a lattice site needs, leaving one bond incomplete (a hole).

Count the complete bonds versus the open hole marker near the acceptor atom.

2. After the acceptor "ionizes," what charge does the fixed acceptor atom carry?

Once a neighboring electron fills the acceptor's incomplete bond, the acceptor atom has one more electron than protons, making it a fixed negative ion — opposite in sign to an ionized donor.

Click "Ionize Acceptor" and look at the sign on the acceptor atom's marker.
