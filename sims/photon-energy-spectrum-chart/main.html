How to Use This Chart

Hovering over the curve
Shows the exact photon energy and wavelength at that point — there are no sliders on this chart since it plots a single fixed equation across the whole spectrum.
Shaded violet band
Marks the visible-light range (380–700 nm) so you can see how narrow it is compared to the full spectrum.
Dashed red line
Marks silicon's 1.12 eV band gap — light above this line carries enough energy per photon for silicon to absorb it.

Challenge Questions

1. Which region of the spectrum has higher photon energy?

Gamma rays have extremely short wavelengths, and since \(E\propto 1/\lambda\), they carry the highest photon energy on the chart.

Hover over both ends of the curve and compare the y-axis values.

2. The dashed silicon band-gap line crosses the curve in which spectral region?

The intersection sits at about 1107 nm, just past the red end of visible light — in the near-infrared.

Look at the red marker dot and read the labeled band directly above it.

3. Using \(E=hc/\lambda\), if wavelength is cut in half, what happens to photon energy?

Energy is inversely proportional to wavelength (no exponent), so halving \(\lambda\) exactly doubles \(E\).

Hover at 700 nm and then at 350 nm and compare the two energy readouts.

4. Which has more photon energy: 400 nm (violet) light or 700 nm (red) light?

Shorter wavelength means higher energy, so 400 nm violet light carries more energy per photon than 700 nm red light.

Hover over both wavelengths inside the shaded visible band and compare.

5. Why can silicon absorb the near-infrared photon (~1107 nm) marked on this chart?

Absorption requires photon energy to meet or exceed the band gap energy — at this marker, the two are exactly equal, so photons at slightly shorter wavelengths (more energy) are absorbed.

Re-read the paragraph on optical absorption just above this chart in the chapter.
