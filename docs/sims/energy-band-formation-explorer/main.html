Guided Exploration & Challenge Questions

Try This

  • Try this: Set N (number of atoms) to a small value like 3, then drag the spacing slider from large (isolated) down to small (equilibrium). Count the individual discrete levels each atomic level splits into.
  • Predict: Before increasing N to 40, predict what the fan of discrete levels will look like at equilibrium spacing compared to N = 3.
  • Observe: Increase N to 40 and confirm the same splitting now looks like two continuous shaded bands rather than countable individual lines.
  • Explore: Watch the top strip of 8 schematic atoms move together as you decrease the spacing slider, and connect that physical picture to the widening fan of split levels below.
  • Read the numbers: At the closest spacing, compare the "Gap between bands" readout to the bandwidths of the two bands. Does the gap stay open, or do the bands overlap?

Connects to Chapter 5: This MicroSim visualizes band formation directly: each isolated atom has the same sharp discrete energy levels, but bringing N atoms together lets their wavefunctions overlap, splitting each level into N closely-spaced states (here modeled as a simple finite tight-binding spectrum, \(E_j = E_0 \pm t(d)\cos\!\big(\tfrac{\pi(j+1)}{N+1}\big)\)). For large N these states are so closely spaced they look like the continuous energy bands the Kronig-Penney model produces exactly, and the same valence-band / conduction-band terminology applies at the equilibrium spacing.

What Each Control Does

Interatomic spacing d slider
Sets the distance between neighboring atoms; smaller d means stronger wavefunction overlap and a wider spread (bandwidth) of split states.
Number of atoms N slider
Sets how many atoms (and therefore how many split states) form each band; larger N looks more like a continuous band.

Challenge Questions

1. At large interatomic spacing (isolated atoms), what does the fan diagram show for each atomic level?

Isolated atoms don't overlap wavefunctions, so there is no coupling (t ≈ 0) and every atom's level sits at the same discrete energy — a single sharp line rather than a split band.

Drag the spacing slider to its largest (rightmost) value and look at how narrow the fan of lines becomes.

2. As interatomic spacing decreases toward the equilibrium value, what happens to the split-level bandwidth?

Smaller spacing means more wavefunction overlap, which increases the coupling strength t(d) and therefore widens the band, 2×t(d).

Drag the spacing slider toward its smallest value and watch the fan of lines spread apart.

3. Why does a large N (many atoms) make the split levels look like a continuous band, while a small N does not?

The band's total width, 2t(d), does not depend strongly on N, but that width is shared among N discrete states — so more atoms means more, more closely spaced states filling the same energy range, which is exactly why a real crystal's ~10²³ atoms produce what looks like a perfectly continuous band.

Compare the vertical tick marks at N = 3 and N = 40 at the same spacing — the total spread (top to bottom) is similar, but the number of ticks is very different.
