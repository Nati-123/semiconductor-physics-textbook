    Quiz: Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

    Test your understanding of intrinsic/extrinsic material and donor/acceptor doping covered in Chapter 7 with these 20 questions.

    Question 1

    What is an intrinsic semiconductor?

    1. A chemically pure semiconductor whose carriers come only from thermal bond-breaking
    2. A semiconductor that has been heavily doped
    3. A semiconductor with no band gap
    4. A metal with semiconductor-like properties
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    An intrinsic semiconductor is chemically pure, so its only carriers come from rare, thermally-broken covalent bonds.

    Concept Tested: Intrinsic Semiconductor

    Question 2

    Why does breaking a covalent bond in an intrinsic semiconductor always create exactly one electron and one hole?

    1. Because they describe the same single event: the freed electron and the vacancy it left behind
    2. Because two separate bonds must break simultaneously
    3. It does not — sometimes only an electron is created
    4. Because the crystal has an equal number of electrons and holes to begin with
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    A broken bond and the vacancy it leaves are two views of the same event, so intrinsic generation always creates one electron and one hole together.

    Concept Tested: Intrinsic Semiconductor

    Question 3

    What defines an extrinsic semiconductor?

    1. It has no band gap
    2. It has been deliberately doped with donor or acceptor atoms
    3. It is always a compound semiconductor
    4. It has exactly equal numbers of electrons and holes always
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    An extrinsic semiconductor has been deliberately doped with donor or acceptor atoms, giving it a carrier population dominated by the dopant rather than thermal generation.

    Concept Tested: Extrinsic Semiconductor

    Question 4

    What distinguishes an elemental semiconductor from a compound semiconductor?

    1. Elemental semiconductors have no valence electrons
    2. Elemental semiconductors use one atomic species; compound semiconductors alternate two or more species
    3. Compound semiconductors are always metals
    4. There is no difference
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Elemental semiconductors (Si, Ge) use a single atomic species; compound semiconductors (GaAs and others) alternate two different species.

    Concept Tested: Elemental Semiconductor

    Question 5

    Which group pairing produces a III-V compound semiconductor like GaAs?

    1. Group II and Group VI
    2. Group III and Group V
    3. Group IV and Group IV
    4. Group I and Group VII
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    A Group III atom and a Group V atom average 4 valence electrons per atom together, matching silicon's covalent-bonding count.

    Concept Tested: Compound Semiconductor

    Question 6

    What is silicon's approximate room-temperature band gap, and is it direct or indirect?

    1. 1.12 eV, indirect
    2. 1.42 eV, direct
    3. 0.66 eV, indirect
    4. 5.5 eV, direct
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    Silicon has an indirect band gap of about 1.12 eV at room temperature.

    Concept Tested: Silicon

    Question 7

    Why was germanium used in the first practical transistors (1947) instead of silicon?

    1. Germanium has a larger band gap than silicon
    2. Germanium could be purified and processed to sufficient quality earlier, and has higher carrier mobility
    3. Silicon did not exist yet in 1947
    4. Germanium is a compound semiconductor
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Germanium could be purified and processed to sufficient quality earlier than silicon, and its higher carrier mobility eased early device operation, making it the material of the first point-contact transistors.

    Concept Tested: Germanium

    Question 8

    Why is gallium arsenide preferred over silicon for LEDs and laser diodes?

    1. GaAs is direct-gap, allowing efficient photon-only emission
    2. GaAs is cheaper than silicon
    3. GaAs has no band gap
    4. Silicon cannot be doped
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    GaAs's direct band gap allows efficient, photon-only band-edge emission, while silicon's indirect gap requires a much less probable phonon-assisted process.

    Concept Tested: Gallium Arsenide

    Question 9

    How many of a donor atom's valence electrons participate in covalent bonds?

    1. 5
    2. 4
    3. 3
    4. 0
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    4 of a donor's 5 valence electrons fill covalent bonds, identical to the host atom; the 5th is left weakly bound.

    Concept Tested: Donor Atom

    Question 10

    What is left behind at a lattice site after a donor atom ionizes?

    1. A hole
    2. A fixed, positively-charged ion
    3. A free electron only, with no atom remaining
    4. A neutral atom
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Once the 5th electron leaves, the donor atom has one more proton than electron, becoming a fixed positive ion.

    Concept Tested: Donor Atom

    Question 11

    How many of an acceptor atom's valence electrons complete covalent bonds?

    1. 4
    2. 5
    3. 3
    4. 0
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: C

    A Group III acceptor atom has only 3 valence electrons, completing only 3 of the 4 bonds a lattice site needs.

    Concept Tested: Acceptor Atom

    Question 12

    What is the sign of a fully ionized acceptor atom's fixed charge?

    1. Positive
    2. Negative
    3. Neutral
    4. It has no charge
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Once a neighboring electron fills the acceptor's incomplete bond, the acceptor has one more electron than proton, becoming a fixed negative ion.

    Concept Tested: Acceptor Atom

    Question 13

    Roughly how does typical extrinsic (doped) carrier concentration compare to intrinsic carrier concentration in silicon at room temperature?

    1. About the same
    2. Extrinsic is 5-8 orders of magnitude larger
    3. Extrinsic is smaller
    4. Exactly double
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Silicon's intrinsic carrier concentration (~10^10 cm^-3) is dwarfed by typical doping levels (~10^15-10^18 cm^-3), a difference of 5 to 8 orders of magnitude.

    Concept Tested: Extrinsic Semiconductor

    Question 14

    What does the hydrogenic ionization energy formula predict happens as the relative dielectric constant \(\varepsilon_r\) increases?

    1. Ionization energy increases
    2. Ionization energy decreases, since \(\varepsilon_r\) appears squared in the denominator
    3. Ionization energy is unaffected by \(\varepsilon_r\)
    4. The formula becomes undefined
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    Since \(E_D\propto(m^*/m_0)/\varepsilon_r^2\), a larger dielectric constant strongly screens the Coulomb attraction and shrinks the predicted ionization energy.

    Concept Tested: Donor Atom

    Question 15

    Approximately how large are typical measured donor/acceptor ionization energies in silicon?

    1. Tens of meV
    2. Several eV
    3. Exactly 13.6 eV
    4. Zero
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    Measured values are typically tens of meV (e.g. ~45 meV for phosphorus or boron in silicon), comparable to room-temperature thermal energy.

    Concept Tested: Acceptor Atom

    Question 16

    Even at a heavy doping concentration of 10^19 cm^-3 in silicon, roughly what fraction of atoms are dopant atoms?

    1. About half
    2. Roughly 1 in 5,000
    3. Almost all of them
    4. Exactly zero
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    With silicon's atomic density of 5×10^22 cm^-3, a doping level of 10^19 cm^-3 gives a ratio of about 5,000 silicon atoms per dopant atom.

    Concept Tested: Extrinsic Semiconductor

    Question 17

    What is an antisite defect, as introduced in this chapter?

    1. A missing atom
    2. The wrong species occupying a lattice site in a compound semiconductor
    3. A broken covalent bond in silicon
    4. A dopant atom that has not yet ionized
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    An antisite defect occurs when a compound semiconductor's species is misplaced — e.g. a Ga atom occupying an As site in GaAs — typically from off-stoichiometry growth.

    Concept Tested: Compound Semiconductor

    Question 18

    Which material has the highest melting point among silicon, germanium, and GaAs?

    1. Germanium
    2. GaAs
    3. Silicon
    4. They are all equal
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: C

    Silicon has the highest melting point (1414°C) of the three, giving it the widest thermal processing window.

    Concept Tested: Silicon

    Question 19

    According to the Varshni equation, what happens to a semiconductor's band gap as temperature increases?

    1. It increases
    2. It decreases
    3. It stays exactly constant
    4. It becomes negative at any positive temperature
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: B

    The Varshni equation predicts a shrinking band gap with rising temperature, due to lattice expansion and increased atomic vibration.

    Concept Tested: Silicon

    Question 20

    Which chapter builds directly on this chapter's donor/acceptor foundation to develop n-type and p-type doping quantitatively?

    1. Chapter 8
    2. Chapter 3
    3. Chapter 1
    4. No later chapter uses this material
    Show Answer

    Correct Answer: A

    Chapter 8, "Doping, Ionization, and Temperature Regimes," builds directly on this chapter's donor/acceptor foundation.

    Concept Tested: Donor Atom
